The composition notebook

How Shostakovich’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a work by Dmitri Shostakovich and not a rediscovered score. Composed on November 16, 2026, for the top-voted request in the Missing Works queue.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the recorded messages, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony in A minor

Shostakovich wrote fifteen symphonies and none is in A minor. This one follows the four-movement plan of several of them: a slow, bleak first movement, a sardonic scherzo, a Largo, and a march finale whose major-key ending is meant to sound forced. It lasts about eleven minutes (11:09 in the site’s playback); his own symphonies in this plan last forty-five minutes or more, so this is an outline at small scale.

The score has fourteen staves at sounding pitch: flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon, horns, trumpets, trombones, timpani, xylophone and strings. There is no snare drum, piccolo, tuba or celesta, all of which he used constantly.

A — E · F E — B♭ A

The theme: a rising fifth, a half step up and back, and a fall through the flattened second degree.

I. Moderato

A minor · 4/4 · 68 bars · about 3:39

Cellos and basses play the theme alone; the violas add a second line; then the first violins take the theme over pulsing violas. A lonely melody for the flute follows over an open fifth and plucked basses, and the clarinet answers. A march rhythm (long, short-short) starts in the basses and timpani. Horns and trumpets take the head of the theme, violins and woodwind push its rising steps upward, the half step E–F is hammered over the dominant, and the full orchestra plays the first half of the theme in octaves with xylophone. It breaks off. The timpani keep the rhythm quietly, the flute plays the whole theme alone, and the movement ends with the theme’s head plucked in the basses and the clarinet sighing the half step, on an A–E fifth with no third.

II. Allegretto

C major with wrong notes · 2/4 · 124 bars · about 1:48

A shrill polka for flute and clarinet over a plucked oom-pah. Its tune keeps landing on notes from the wrong key (E flat, then F sharp over an A flat bass). The full orchestra repeats it with xylophone; strings scurry in C minor and again a minor third higher; trumpets play a mock fanfare with a flattened upper neighbour. The trio, in A flat, is a clownish tune for the bassoon, then for clarinet and flute, with a passage of violins sliding down in parallel sixths. When the polka returns its tune is in the xylophone, plucked violins and a high bassoon. The ending is curt.

III. Largo

D minor · 4/4 · 42 bars · about 3:14

The strings alone play an eight-bar chorale, with a Neapolitan E flat chord in its second half and a close on D major. The oboe plays a lament under high held violins, with plucked basses. Flute and clarinet take the lament’s first phrase in canon a bar apart while the strings grow louder, and the full orchestra plays the chorale with trumpets on the tune. The sound drains away over a held D. The flute remembers the lament over the chorale’s harmony, and the movement ends on a quiet D major chord.

IV. Allegro non troppo

A minor, ending in A major · 4/4 · 74 bars · about 2:28

After two bars of timpani and basses, trumpets and trombones play a march whose first notes are the first movement’s theme (A, E), over strings hammering the harmony in eighth notes; its second phrase stands on B flat, the flattened second degree. Violins and woodwind add a second strain. The march is played quietly by the violins; cellos and horn recall the first movement’s flute melody over the march rhythm; a short fugato on the head of the march climbs by fifths; the march and second strain return. In the slower coda the brass broaden the head of the theme and turn it to A major while every other instrument repeats the note A, more than a hundred times in the first violins, before the last chord. The brass pass through B flat against those repeated A’s.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Shostakovich or anyone else, and his musical signature D–E flat–C–B (DSCH) is not used.
  • The Fifth Symphony is the main model, and the debts are direct: a Moderato that grows from quiet string writing to a march climax and ends quietly; a Largo with a string chorale and an oboe solo under high violins; and a finale that begins with a brass march over timpani and ends in the major with one note repeated by most of the orchestra. In the Fifth that note is A, the fifth of D major. Here it is also A, as the keynote of A major. The resemblance is intended and is the homage; the themes are different.
  • The polka with wrong notes, xylophone and a comic bassoon follows the manner of the Ninth Symphony, the finale of the Sixth and the Polka from The Golden Age.
  • The long, short-short rhythm, the flattened second and fourth degrees, bare two-part writing, and solo woodwind lines over almost nothing are habits found throughout his symphonies and quartets.

How it was written and revised

  • The movements were written by hand in a compact private source in which every note carries its sounding pitch. A build step writes the fourteen-staff score, fills silent parts with rests, creates doublings from one written line, and reuses blocks for returns. In the finale a small helper spells out the repeated-note bars; the pitches were chosen by hand.
  • Six playable draft revisions were published, starting with the first sixteen bars; the last matches the published score.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the polka’s return was a literal repeat and now gives the tune to xylophone, plucked violins and a high bassoon; the Largo’s canon now grows in volume into the full-orchestra chorale instead of jumping to it.
  • Things the checks caught and that were fixed before publication: a xylophone doubling an octave above its range, and a few places in the Largo where a long note in the lament sat a half step from a held string note.
  • Balance: the printed dynamics of wind, brass and double-bass parts are one to three levels below the strings, using the adjustment measured for an earlier orchestral work on this site. After the first movement was finished each of its fourteen parts was rendered alone; their levels were similar (raw RMS over the whole movement between 0.012 and 0.026 for every part except timpani and xylophone, which play little). These marks are a playback compromise, not dynamics for live players. The other three movements were not measured part by part.

What was tested

  • A checker reads the built score and reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and minor seconds or ninths held for a half note or more (a looser limit than for the earlier works, because short half-step clashes are part of this style). It also compares its reading, note by note, with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors and no warnings.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every published revision.
  • All four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser with all fourteen parts, including the trombone and xylophone samples, which no earlier work on the site uses. Each engraves, plays with no notation warnings or console errors, and has no silent second.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony. Whether the scherzo sounds sardonic or merely odd, and whether the ending sounds forced or just loud, are exactly the things the checks cannot tell.
  • The scale is small. The first movement has no real development section, and the long spans of tension that define his slow movements are not attempted.
  • The orchestration has not been tried by players. The timpani part needs several retunings (A, E, C, G, D across the movements), and the brass sit high for long stretches in the finale.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples: one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos, a generic plucked-string sample, single timpani strokes with no rolls, and no snare drum. Accents have little effect, so the march rhythms are flatter than written.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 2/4, 4/4 and 4/4 here).
