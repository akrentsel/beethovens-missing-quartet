The composition notebook

How Beethoven’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a rediscovered work by Ludwig van Beethoven. Composed on November 16, 2026, for a request in the Missing Works voting queue.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the recorded messages, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

One motto, minor to major

This is a compact symphony in F minor in four movements, about ten and three-quarter minutes (10:43 in the site’s playback). Beethoven’s own symphonies last from twenty-five minutes to more than an hour, so this is a symphony in outline. It is scored for the orchestra of his first eight symphonies without trombones: flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons, horns, trumpets, timpani and strings, on twelve staves at sounding pitch.

F · G A♭ C   D♭ — C

The motto: a dotted rise through the tonic chord to D flat, which sighs down to C.

The first movement is built on this motto. The finale turns it to the major (F, G, A, C, D, C) and makes it the main theme, so the sigh from D flat to C becomes a whole step from D to C.

I. Allegro con brio

F minor · 4/4 · 124 bars · about 3:46

The full orchestra gives the motto in octaves, then the violins repeat it quietly over pulsing violas and a falling scale closes the paragraph. The second theme, in A flat, is a long-breathed melody for clarinet and bassoon and then for violins and flute; under it the cellos keep playing the motto very quietly. The exposition closes with the motto in A flat and hammered chords.

The development states the motto in D flat and B flat minor, then on G with full force. A fugato in C minor follows, with the motto as subject entering in cellos and basses, violas, second violins and first violins. Over a long dominant pedal the sigh passes between woodwind and strings, louder each time. In the recapitulation the second theme and the closing return in F major, and the coda brings the motto back in F minor to end the movement.

II. Andante con moto

D flat major · 2/4 · 64 bars · about 2:13

A sixteen-bar hymn-like theme for the strings. In the first variation flute and clarinet take the theme while the cellos run in eighth notes and the other strings pluck the harmony. An episode gives the theme’s first half in the minor (written as C sharp minor) for the full orchestra and stops on the dominant. The second variation is for the full orchestra with the cello line bowed. The clarinet remembers the theme in a short coda with a plagal close.

III. Scherzo: Allegro vivace

F minor · 3/4 · 120 bars · about 1:40

A clipped staccato tune, first hushed in the violins, then hammered by the full orchestra with a chord on every beat. It moves to A flat and B flat minor and returns over a dominant pedal. The trio, in F major, is led by the two horns in sixths, fifths and thirds and answered by the clarinets, with a second strain for the strings. After the scherzo’s return a coda breaks the tune off in the basses and violins before two bars of F minor.

IV. Finale: Allegro molto

F major · 4/4 · 124 bars · about 3:04

The motto in the major opens the finale in the full orchestra and is repeated quietly by the woodwind over plucked strings. The second theme, in C major, is given to clarinet and bassoon and then to the violins. The development passes the motto’s opening, in its minor form, through D minor, B flat, G minor and E flat, then sets it over the dominant with its minor sixth; the first movement’s sigh from D flat to C is heard once more, quietly, before a scale leads back. The recapitulation brings the second theme in F major, and a Presto coda ends the symphony with the motto twice more and plain tonic and dominant chords.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Beethoven or anyone else.
  • The plan follows the Fifth Symphony in several ways, and these are deliberate: a motto in octaves at the start, the motto continuing in the basses under the second theme, a variation slow movement, and a finale that turns the minor into the major. The motto itself is different in rhythm and shape from the Fifth’s four-note figure.
  • F minor is the key of the Egmont overture, the “Appassionata” Sonata and the Quartet op. 95. The fall from the flat sixth degree to the fifth is one of Beethoven’s most frequent minor-key gestures.
  • A trio led by the horns follows the example of the “Eroica” Symphony, which uses three horns; here there are two.
  • Unlike the Fifth, the scherzo is not linked to the finale and is not recalled in it; the finale recalls the first movement’s sigh instead.

How it was written and revised

  • The movements were written in a compact private source in which every note carries its sounding pitch. A build step writes the twelve-staff score, fills silent parts with rests, creates doublings from one written line, and reuses blocks for recapitulations and the scherzo’s return, transposed where needed. The slow movement’s variations are generated from the four written lines of its theme.
  • Six playable draft revisions were published during the work, beginning with the exposition of the first movement; the last matches the published score.
  • After the first complete draft the scherzo received its coda and sforzando marks were added to the motto’s sighs in the outer movements.
  • Balance: the printed dynamics of wind, brass and double-bass parts are one to three levels below the strings, using the adjustment measured for the previous orchestral work on this site, which uses the same samples. These marks are a playback compromise, not dynamics for live players. The parts of this symphony were not measured one by one.
  • Things the checks caught and that were fixed before publication: bassoon and flute doublings outside their ranges after transposition, and about ten places where a melodic sigh or passing note sat a semitone from a held or hammered note for a quarter note or longer.

What was tested

  • A checker reads the built score and reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and minor seconds or ninths held for a quarter note or more. It also compares its reading, note by note, with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors and no warnings.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every published revision.
  • All four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser with all twelve parts. Each engraves, plays with no notation warnings or console errors, and has no silent second.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony.
  • The orchestration has not been tried by players. Ranges were checked against simple limits only. The timpani are tuned to F and C in three movements and would need retuning to D flat and A flat for the Andante.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples: one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos, a generic plucked-string sample, and single timpani strokes. Accents and sforzando marks have little or no effect on the sound.
  • The writing is plain: much of the tutti doubles one line in octaves over repeated-note harmony, the variations keep the theme’s harmony unchanged, and the developments are short. Beethoven’s inner parts and his way of stretching a development are not attempted at scale.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 here).
