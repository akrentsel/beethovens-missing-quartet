The composition notebook

How Bach’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Johann Sebastian Bach. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony Bach could not have written

Johann Sebastian Bach wrote no symphony. The word “sinfonia” in his music means an instrumental opening movement of a cantata or a three-part keyboard invention; the multi-movement concert symphony belongs to his sons’ generation. This piece is an honest counterfactual: it asks what a four-movement orchestral work might look like if it were assembled from the forms he did use for orchestra, above all the four Ouvertures (orchestral suites). It lasts about eleven minutes (11:02 in the site’s playback).

The band is the festive D major ensemble of the third and fourth suites, reduced: two natural trumpets, timpani, two oboes, bassoon, strings and harpsichord. The score has twelve staves and is written at sounding pitch. The trumpet parts use only notes of the harmonic series of D, and the timpani only D and A, as the instruments of his time required.

D · F♯ A · D′ — C♯ B

The head of the fugue subject of the first movement: a rising triad to the upper keynote, and two steps down.

I. Ouverture: Grave – Vivace – Grave

D major · 4/4 · 69 bars, the opening Grave repeated · about 3:53

A French overture. The Grave is in the dotted rhythm of the genre, for the whole band, moving from D to the dominant in twelve bars; it is repeated. The Vivace is a four-voice fugue. Its three-bar subject rises through the triad, then turns twice in sixteenth notes and closes on the fifth. It enters in the second violins, first violins, basses and violas, with a countersubject that runs where the subject stands still. An episode for the two oboes and bassoon alone passes the subject’s turning figure down a circle of fifths; entries follow in B minor, F sharp minor and, in the basses, G major, separated by an episode for the strings. Over a dominant pedal the head of the subject enters a bar apart in violas, second violins and first violins; the last entry has the whole subject in the first trumpet. Eight bars of the Grave return to close.

II. Siciliano: Andante

B minor · 6/8 · 24 bars, both halves repeated · about 2:11

Trumpets, drums, second oboe and bassoon are silent. A solo oboe sings in the lilting dotted rhythm of the siciliano over strings and a harpsichord that breaks each chord into single notes. The first half moves from B minor to D major; the second passes through E minor and F sharp minor, and when the tune returns the first violins play it with the oboe an octave lower. The first violins answer the oboe at the two cadences.

III. Menuet I – Menuet II – Menuet I

D major and D minor · 3/4 · 72 bars · about 2:44

The first minuet is for the full band, with trumpets and drums marking the main cadences; its second half touches B minor. The second minuet, in D minor, is a trio for two oboes and bassoon with a quiet harpsichord. Both are in two repeated halves; the first minuet then returns without repeats.

IV. Gigue: Allegro

D major · 6/8 · 56 bars, both halves repeated · about 2:14

A gigue that begins like a fugue: a four-bar subject of leaping eighth notes in the first violins, answered by the second violins and then by the basses and bassoon, before the full band takes over. As in many of Bach’s gigues the second half begins with the subject turned upside down. It passes through B minor, oboes and violins trade single bars, the subject returns in D with the first trumpet (every note of it lies in the trumpet’s harmonic series), its first bar is thrown from trumpet to violins to basses, and a dominant pedal leads to the close.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Bach or anyone else. The subjects, countersubjects and tunes were written for this piece.
  • The plan follows the orchestral suites: overture with fugue, a quiet movement for reduced forces, paired dances “alternativement”, a gigue last. The scoring with trumpets and drums follows the third and fourth suites; the oboe-and-bassoon trio follows the wind trios in the first and fourth.
  • The debts are to manner, not to themes: dotted Grave, invertible subject and countersubject, episodes on circle-of-fifths sequences, a stretto over a dominant pedal, and the inverted subject after the double bar of a gigue.
  • The best-known slow movement in these suites, the Air of the third, is in D major over a walking bass. To stay away from it, this slow movement is a B minor siciliano for oboe.

How it was written and revised

  • The subjects, countersubjects, themes, episodes, trumpet and timpani parts and the chord of every beat were written by hand in a compact private source in which every note carries its sounding pitch. A program then writes the score: it creates doublings (oboes with violins, bassoon and double bass with the cellos), realises the harpsichord right hand, and writes the inner filling parts from the chord plan with the smoothest motion it can find.
  • Four playable drafts were published while the work was written, starting with the twelve-bar Grave.
  • The first builds showed sustained half-step clashes where the fugue’s countersubject met the program’s filling parts; the subject’s harmony was changed to fit. The return of the first minuet had been read in the wrong key signature and was corrected.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: a check for parallel octaves and fifths between melody and bass found 47 across the four movements, most in the minuets, whose basses had been written by the program. Both minuet basses were rewritten by hand, the fugue’s entries were re-harmonised, the top line of the fugue’s close was replaced, and about twenty bars of melody were changed in the Grave, the Siciliano, the minuets and the Gigue. After the revision the check finds none between melody and bass. The first violins were also given answers to the oboe at the Siciliano’s cadences.

What was tested

  • A checker reads the source and reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes between parts that last a quarter note or more. It also compares its reading, note by note, with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors and no warnings.
  • The trumpet parts were checked against the harmonic series of the natural trumpet in D.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every published revision.
  • All four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser. Each engraves, starts playing within four seconds, produces no notation warnings or console errors, and its rendered audio has no silent second. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony.
  • The inner string parts and the harpsichord are filling written by a program from a chord plan. In a real Bach fugue every voice is a melody; here only the subject, the countersubject and the bass are. A few parallel octaves remain between the second violins and the bass.
  • The fugue is short and its episodes are simple sequences; there is no augmentation, and the stretto uses only the head of the subject.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players (mostly forte for the band, softer for the trios). They were not altered to balance the samples; in playback the harpsichord and oboes may sound stronger, and the strings weaker, than a real ensemble. Individual parts can be muted in the player.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples: one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos, a modern trumpet for the natural trumpets, single timpani strokes. Trills are played by the site’s player; other ornaments a Baroque player would add are not written.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 6/8, 3/4 and 6/8 here).
