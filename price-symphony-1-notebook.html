The composition notebook

How Price’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Florence Price. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A fifth symphony in her manner

Florence Price wrote four symphonies. The First, in E minor, was played by the Chicago Symphony Orchestra in 1933, the first symphony by a Black American woman performed by a major orchestra. The Second, in G minor, is lost; the Third and Fourth, in C minor and D minor, survive. This piece is a new symphony in A flat major in her manner. It is not a reconstruction of the lost Second or a completion of anything of hers, and no theme is taken from her. It lasts about fourteen minutes (14:01 in the site’s playback).

The orchestra: pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, four horns, two trumpets, three trombones and tuba, timpani and strings, with chimes in the slow movement, on thirteen or fourteen staves at sounding pitch.

What is taken from her way of writing

  • Themes on the five-note (pentatonic) scale, in the manner of spirituals. All the themes here were newly written; no spiritual or other existing melody is quoted.
  • Call and response between solo instruments.
  • A slow movement that is a hymn for the brass choir, answered by the woodwind, with chimes.
  • A “Juba” as third movement: the syncopated dance, with its patted off-beats, that she put where a European symphony has a scherzo.
  • A short, fast finale in six-eight time.

A♭ — B♭ — C — E♭ — F

The pentatonic scale on A flat, from which the first movement’s horn call and main theme, the Juba tune and the finale’s theme are made.

I. Andante – Allegro ma non troppo

A flat major · 4/4 · 144 bars, exposition repeated · about 6:44

A solo horn calls on the five notes of the scale over held strings; the strings answer; horns and clarinets repeat the call, growing. The Allegro’s main theme is a broad pentatonic melody for the violins, repeated by the full orchestra. A transition darkens to C minor, where the second theme is given as call and response: an oboe calls for two bars, a clarinet responds for two. The strings and flute sing the same melody whole, and a closing theme in E flat ends the exposition.

The development brings back the horn call in D flat at the new speed, gives the main theme in F and B flat, and lets its opening turns rise over a held E flat until they fall silent. In the reprise the call and response returns in F minor, for clarinet and bassoon. The coda gives the horn call to the whole orchestra and ends with two quiet “Amen” cadences.

II. Largo

D flat major · 4/4 · 48 bars · about 3:33

A newly written eight-bar hymn for trumpets, horns and trombones alone. The woodwind respond with four bars of their own. The hymn is played again with the strings marking the beats beneath, and the woodwind respond again. In the middle a clarinet sings over the strings in B flat minor, and violins and flute lead back. The hymn returns for the whole orchestra with chimes, and the movement ends with two “Amen” cadences.

III. Juba: Allegro

A flat major, trio in D flat · 2/4 · 68 bars, first part repeated · about 2:00

The Juba tune has a short note, a long one across the beat, and a short one, over an accompaniment in which the basses give the beats and the other strings the off-beats, like hands patting. It is played by the violins and the full orchestra, with a strain in F minor for clarinet and flute between. The trio is a smooth melody in D flat for the violins over the same steady beat, joined by the oboe. The Juba returns in oboe and flute and then for everyone.

IV. Finale: Presto

A flat major · 6/8 · 104 bars · about 1:44

A rondo in constant motion. The theme, for violins and then full orchestra, runs up and down the pentatonic scale. The first episode is in F minor for clarinet and flute; the second is a broad melody in long notes in D flat, over the running accompaniment, with oboe and clarinets joining for its second half. The theme’s first phrase is heard in D flat and E flat, eight bars on the dominant thin out to silence, and after the last return the coda ends with the “Amen” cadence of the first two movements, now fast and loud.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Price, from any spiritual or hymn, or from anyone else. A pentatonic tune that moves by step and third will resemble many spirituals in general shape; none was used as a model.
  • The plan follows her First and Third Symphonies: a sonata first movement with a spiritual-like second theme, a slow movement built on a brass hymn, a Juba, a fast compound-time finale.
  • The harmony is the late-Romantic harmony of her teachers’ generation and of Dvořák, who had urged American composers towards exactly these sources.

How it was written and revised

  • Every tune, the hymn, the call-and-response layout and the chord of every bar were written by hand. A program then scores quiet, woodwind and full-orchestra passages from the chord plan: accompaniment figures, tremolo, wind and brass chords, timpani.
  • Four playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • The first build of the first movement reported 68 half-step clashes. Nearly all had one cause: pentatonic melodies dwell on the sixth above the bass, and the accompaniment was adding a seventh to the same chord. The chords were simplified to plain triads in those bars. The same thing happened once in the hymn and was fixed the same way. Sixteen parallel octaves or fifths between melody and bass were removed by changing inversions.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: chimes were added to the hymn’s last statement and closing cadences, as in the slow movement of her First Symphony, and clarinets were added an octave below the finale’s broad melody.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes lasting a quarter note or more, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report none. A second check counts parallel fifths and octaves between first violins and bass: none remain where the violins have the tune; three remain in the first movement where they accompany the call and response. Timpani parts were checked against their tuning in each movement.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony.
  • Price’s melodies come from a living tradition she knew from the inside. These are pentatonic tunes written to a description of that tradition, and the difference will be audible to anyone who knows her music.
  • The Juba’s character depends on accent and swing, which the sampled playback barely renders; there is no percussion, where she sometimes used African drums and wind whistle.
  • The accompaniment is written by a program in a few textures and is regular. Dynamics are terraced and written as intended for players, not altered to balance the samples; the brass hymn may sound heavier or lighter than intended.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples, with a tubular-bell sample for the chimes. The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 4/4, 2/4 and 6/8 here).
