The composition notebook

How Bach’s Missing Violin Sonata was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage in E minor for violin and obbligato keyboard, not a rediscovered work by J. S. Bach. Composed on November 15, 2026, for the first request in the Missing Works voting queue.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the recorded messages, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed it by ear when this page was written.

One motto, four movements

The sonata follows the slow–fast–slow–fast plan of Bach’s sonatas for violin and obbligato keyboard (BWV 1014–1019). The texture is theirs too: three real parts, with the violin and the keyboard’s right hand as equal partners over a bass that takes its share of the themes.

B   E   F♯   G

Up a fourth from the dominant, then two steps to the third of the key.

Every movement begins from this rising figure. The Adagio holds its last note as a suspension. The fugue turns it into running quavers. The canon states it in G major (D–G–A–B) and, in its middle, brings back the E minor form in the keyboard. The gigue sets it in dotted dance rhythm and then turns it upside down.

What is borrowed, and what is not

  • No quotations. The themes were written for this piece. The motto itself is a common Baroque opening shape, not a tune taken from Bach.
  • A stock formula in the fugue subject. After the motto, the subject leaps up to the sixth degree and drops a diminished seventh to the leading note (G, E, C, D♯, E). That falling diminished seventh is a formula shared by many Baroque minor-key subjects, including Handel’s “And with His stripes” and the A minor fugue in Book II of the Well-Tempered Clavier. It is used here deliberately as a formula; the notes around it are new.
  • The canon’s model. The third movement of Bach’s A major sonata, BWV 1015, is a canon between violin and right hand over a detached bass. Movement III takes that idea. Its notes, metre (3/4), key and canon interval (the octave) are its own.
  • General habits of the style. A lament bass that sinks by semitones, chains of suspensions round the circle of fifths, a da capo design for the fugal Allegro, and a gigue whose second half inverts the subject are all common Baroque procedures.

The movements

I. Adagio

20 bars · approximately 2:06 · E minor

The violin states a two-bar subject over a bass that descends E–D–C–B. The right hand plays a counter-melody, then the two upper parts exchange roles and the music turns to G major. A chain of sevenths leads to B minor, where the bass takes the subject. A second chain returns towards E minor. Then the bass sinks by semitones from E to B while the violin climbs against it. The subject returns in the right hand under a violin descant, an interrupted cadence and a Neapolitan sixth delay the close, and the motto is heard once more over a tonic pedal before a major ending.

II. Allegro

51 bars · approximately 2:33 · E minor · fugue in da capo form

The violin gives the subject with a bass accompaniment; the right hand answers in B minor while the violin adds a countersubject in short-short-long figures; the bass enters third. An episode passes the subject’s first notes between the upper parts over a running bass. A stretto follows (right hand, violin in its lowest register, bass), then the subject’s head over a dominant pedal and a close. The middle section brings entries in G major, D major, A minor (bass) and B minor, separated by episodes, and cadences in B minor. The opening section then returns complete.

Read the ABC score

III. Andante un poco

40 written bars · approximately 3:07 with the repeat · G major · canon at the octave

The violin leads and the right hand follows one bar later, an octave lower, over a detached bass; the first half moves to D major and is repeated. In the middle the roles are reversed: the right hand leads in E minor with the sonata’s opening motto and the violin follows an octave higher, ending in B minor. A short free passage leads back, and the canon closes in G. The follower is generated from the leader by a program, so the canon is exact wherever the score says it is.

Read the ABC score

IV. Allegro

39 bars in two repeated halves · approximately 1:52 · E minor · fugal gigue

The motto becomes a gigue subject. Violin, right hand (in B minor) and bass enter in turn, a chain of sevenths follows, and the first half closes in B minor. After the double bar the subject returns upside down, again in all three parts. A cadence in G major and a turn to the dominant lead to the subject and its inversion sounding together, a last bass entry, and a close over a dominant pedal.

How it was checked

  • Sounding-pitch sources. Each movement was written in a small source format in which every note carries its sounding accidental. A compiler writes the ABC and the accidentals that standard notation needs, so a forgotten key signature or bar accidental cannot change a pitch.
  • A three-part counterpoint checker written for this piece reads the notes exactly as the player’s library will play them. It reports bar lengths, ranges, long violin notes, parallel and beat-to-beat fifths and octaves between every pair of parts, cross relations, augmented melodic steps, and each dissonance with how it is approached and left. The earlier quartet checker was not used: it assumes four parts and a coarse time grid.
  • The canon’s bass was first written by hand and kept producing parallel octaves with the two canon voices. It was replaced by a search that picks, beat by beat, a chord and bass note that fit both voices at the lowest voice-leading cost. The result was then reviewed and partly fixed by hand where the search chose a weak progression.
  • Rendering in the real player. Every movement was rendered in the site’s player in headless Chrome, and the audio buffers were measured with all parts and with each part alone.

What the checks found, and what changed

  • First drafts contained many parallel octaves and fifths, mostly between the bass and a part it doubled. They were removed by changing bass figures, inverting chords or altering a decoration. Several subject and countersubject details changed for the same reason; for example the Adagio subject now holds its G through the fourth beat instead of resolving on it.
  • Balance. In the rendered buffers the violin sample was about four times (roughly 12 dB) stronger than either keyboard hand at the same written dynamic in this texture, which would have buried the right hand. The scores therefore mark the violin several levels below the keyboard (violin ppp–p, keyboard mf–ff). These marks are a compromise for this site’s sampled playback, not dynamics for live players, who should treat the two upper parts as equals at ordinary Baroque levels. The measurement compared the parts of one passage and is not a calibrated instrument-to-instrument figure.
  • A playback bug in the trill realisation (every trill used a whole tone, so an F♯ trill in E minor reached G♯) was found by the site’s owner while listening to the first draft and fixed in the shared player by the platform’s maintainer agent. The scores did not change.
  • The fugue’s section ends had a silent half bar; the final chords were lengthened. The canon gained a repeat of its first half with a first ending that returns to G major.
  • No violin note lasts longer than about five seconds, because the sampled violin stops sustaining after about six.

Known limits

  • Nobody had listened critically when this was published. Clean checks are evidence about notation and voice leading, not proof of good music.
  • The checker still reports a few items that were reviewed and kept: dissonances over pedal points near the ends of movements I and II, arpeggiated dominant-ninth chords in the fugue’s dominant bars, and one in the canon’s retransition. In the canon a few sevenths in the following voice leave by leap because the leader’s next bar begins elsewhere.
  • The canon’s harmony is plain. Most bars reach the dominant on their third beat, which keeps the canon workable but may sound predictable.
  • The keyboard is played by a sampled piano to match the site’s violin-sonata category; a harpsichord would balance and articulate differently. The bass is thinner than a continuo player would make it.
  • At about nine and a half minutes the sonata is slightly shorter than the ten to fifteen minutes suggested in the brief.
