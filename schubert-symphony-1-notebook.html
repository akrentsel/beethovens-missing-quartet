The composition notebook

How Schubert’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Franz Schubert. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony in a key he left alone

Franz Schubert finished seven symphonies and left others in fragments, the “Unfinished” in B minor among them. None is in A major, the key of the “Trout” Quintet and two of his piano sonatas. This is a new four-movement symphony in that key. It is not a completion of anything he sketched, and no theme is his. It lasts about sixteen minutes (15:47 in the site’s playback).

The orchestra is that of his earlier symphonies: pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons, horns and trumpets, timpani and strings, without trombones. The score has twelve staves at sounding pitch. Horns and trumpets are written as natural instruments in A (the horns in F for the slow movement).

A major → C major → E major · A major → F major → A major

The keys of the first movement’s exposition and reprise. Each time, the second theme enters a third below the key that closes the section.

I. Allegro moderato

A major · 4/4 · 156 bars, exposition repeated · about 7:14

Two bars of murmuring strings over a held horn note, then a song for the clarinet that closes on the dominant. The violins take it up and darken its end by lowering one note, F sharp to F natural: the major–minor shading that returns throughout the symphony. A tutti in dotted rhythm ends on a bare E held by everyone. From that E the cellos begin the second theme, quietly, in C major, a third below the expected key; clarinet and flute repeat it, and four bars lift the music to E major for the closing theme.

The development gives the clarinet’s song to the oboe in A minor and to the clarinet in F, lets the dotted rhythm storm through D minor and A minor, and remembers the cello theme very quietly, in F for the flute and in D minor for the cellos. Eight bars wait on the dominant. In the reprise the tutti ends on a bare A, and the cello theme enters in F major, again a third below; the closing theme brings back A. Only in the coda do the cellos play their theme in the home key, before the clarinet’s song and a quiet ending.

II. Andante con moto

F major · 2/4 · 96 bars · about 2:54

F major is itself a third below the symphony’s key. An oboe sings a walking tune over a stepping bass; clarinet and flute answer; a second strain for the violins is shadowed by F minor and D flat. A storm in D minor for the full orchestra frames a lyrical answer in B flat for the clarinet. The song returns over running sixteenth notes, the lyrical answer comes back in the home key with a bassoon below the clarinet, and the movement ends with the song very quietly. Trumpets and drums are silent.

III. Scherzo: Allegro vivace – Trio

A major, Trio in D · 3/4 · 136 bars · about 3:16

A quick scherzo, one beat to the bar: a quiet tune of rising broken chords that the full orchestra carries to E major. The second part drops without warning into C major, very quietly, gathers over a held E, falls into a silent bar, and returns to the tune. The Trio is a slower ländler in D for the clarinet over a waltzing accompaniment, the flute joining an octave higher when it returns. The scherzo is then played again without repeats.

IV. Finale: Allegro vivace

A major · 2/4 · 172 bars · about 2:23

A sonata-rondo in constant eighth-note motion. The violins’ theme is repeated by the full orchestra; the transition keeps running until it settles on E, where oboe, then clarinet and flute, sing a slow-moving second theme over the strings’ off-beats. After the closing figure the rondo theme returns. The development puts it in A minor (oboe) and C major (flute) and drives the transition’s motion through F and D minor. In the reprise the song comes in A, and the coda lets the motion dwindle to a whisper before three loud chords.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Schubert or anyone else, and nothing here is based on his symphonic sketches.
  • The habits are his: themes that are songs; a second theme in an unexpected key reached through one held note (the “three-key exposition”); keys a third apart; the same melody turned from major to minor by one changed note; accompaniment figures that keep going under the tune; a ländler as trio; a finale that does not stop moving.
  • A quiet opening murmur before a woodwind theme, and a second theme given to the cellos, are also features of the “Unfinished” Symphony. The resemblance of layout is intended; the key, metre and melodies are different.

How it was written and revised

  • Every tune, the chord of every bar and the form of each movement were written by hand. A program then scores each passage in one of three textures (a tune over quiet strings, a woodwind band, the full orchestra) and writes the accompaniment figures, tremolo, wind chords and natural brass and drum parts from the chord plan. Reprises were made by transposing the exposition’s sections.
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Checks during writing found about forty parallel octaves or fifths between first violins and bass across the four movements; they were removed by changing chord inversions and the last note of a number of bars. The reprise of the first movement’s closing theme ran above the violins’ range and was lowered an octave.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the cello theme had been heard only in C and in F, so four bars were added at the start of the coda in which the cellos play it in A major; the horns were given a held note under the opening murmur.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes lasting a quarter note or more, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report none. A second check counts parallel fifths and octaves between first violins and bass: none remain where the violins have the tune. (It reports two in the four added coda bars, where the first violins accompany and briefly double the cello melody.)
  • Horn, trumpet and timpani parts were checked against the notes of the natural instruments.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony. Whether the tunes sing is the first thing a listener would judge and the last thing these checks can.
  • The accompaniment is written by a program in a few textures, so it is uniform: the same pulsing figure under every quiet tune, the same tremolo in every tutti. The woodwind seldom answer one another, and the inner strings have no melodic life.
  • Schubert’s long paragraphs, in which a modulation is delayed and savoured, are only outlined; each section here is eight bars.
  • There are no slurs, accents or gradual dynamics; dynamics are terraced and written as intended for players, not altered to balance the samples.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples, with one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos, so the cello theme does not sound like a cello section. The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 2/4, 3/4 and 2/4 here).
