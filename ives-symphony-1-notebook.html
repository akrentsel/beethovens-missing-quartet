The composition notebook

How Ives’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Charles Ives. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

Three tunes that nobody knows

Charles Ives wrote four numbered symphonies. What makes them his is the use of music his neighbours already carried in their heads — gospel hymns, quicksteps, fiddle tunes — quoted, bent, and piled on top of one another in different keys and at different speeds, the way two bands sound when they pass on a parade ground.

A homage in this library cannot do that with real tunes: the rule here is new thematic material, not recognisable existing melodies. So the first step was to write three plain tunes of my own in those manners, and the second was to treat them as Ives treated borrowed ones. That is the honest difference between this piece and its model. Ives’s listeners recognised what was colliding; here the tunes are strangers, and the first movement and the start of the second exist partly to teach them to the listener before they are layered. Nothing is quoted from Ives, from any hymnal, or from any march. The piece lasts about eight minutes (8:02 in the site’s playback).

hymn in F · march in C · fiddle tune in D

The three tunes and their home keys. In the collisions they are also heard in A flat, D flat and E flat.

The orchestra: pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, four horns, two trumpets, three trombones, timpani, tubular bells and strings, on fourteen staves at sounding pitch.

I. Andante

F major, with other keys laid over it · 4/4 · 38 bars · about 2:18

The hymn tune is played quietly by the first violins over a plain string harmony, then by cellos and bassoon with a flute descant. Horns take it in F while oboes and clarinets follow a bar later with the same tune in A flat. Then everything at once: the hymn in F in trumpets, trombones and violins, the clarinets still in A flat, the march tune in D flat in flutes and oboes, timpani and bells. It stops. The strings finish the hymn alone, a flute remembers its opening in the wrong key, and the last chord is left open.

II. Allegro

C major, with D, F, E flat and A flat against it · 2/4 · 78 bars · about 1:18

The march, for trumpets and then the whole band, with a trio strain in F for clarinets and horns. A fiddle tune breaks in, in D, in violins and flutes, while basses and trombones keep the march’s harmony in C. Then three keys at once: the fiddle tune in D, the basses in C, the trio strain in F in the clarinets, with bells. Then both bands: the march in C in the trumpets, the fiddle tune now in E flat, and the hymn in A flat in horns and trombones. Silence; a single clarinet tries the hymn a half step too high; the march crashes back and ends on a chord that belongs to no one key.

III. Largo

G major strings, with horn and woodwind outside the key · 4/4 · 28 bars · about 2:26

Brass (except one horn), timpani and bells are silent. The strings play a four-bar chorale as quietly as possible, seven times, without any change. Above them a horn asks the same five-note question five times. Flute, oboe and clarinet answer four times: first in calm quarter notes, then in eighths, in triplets, and in loud sixteenth notes. The fifth question gets no answer, and the strings go on as before.

IV. Finale: Maestoso

F major, with C and D against it · 4/4 · 38 bars · about 2:00

The first half of the hymn becomes a fugato: cellos, violas, second violins and first violins enter two bars apart, alternately in F and C. Trumpets and horns play the whole hymn in F while the woodwind play the march in C against it and the bells ring. Then all three tunes together: the hymn in trombones and horns (and in A flat in the oboes), the march in the trumpets, the fiddle tune in D in violins, flutes and clarinets. The orchestra slides down through whole-tone chords. Far away, the strings play the end of the hymn in A flat and then in F; a flute remembers the tune in a distant key; bells; and the last F major chord has one note in the flute, B natural, that does not belong.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Ives or from any existing hymn, march or dance tune. The three tunes were written for this piece and kept deliberately plain. A hymn-like tune that moves by step within a fifth will resemble many real hymns in general shape; none was used as a model.
  • The methods are his: a tune answered by itself in another key, two bands at once, a tune “remembered wrongly” by a single instrument, a loud pile-up cut off to reveal quiet strings, an ending left open.
  • The third movement takes its whole idea from “The Unanswered Question”: unchanging quiet strings, a repeated question from a brass instrument, increasingly agitated woodwind answers, a last question unanswered. That resemblance is intended and is the homage; the chorale, the question and the answers are new notes, and the question is given to a horn rather than a trumpet.
  • A fugue on a hymn is the third movement of his Fourth Symphony; here it is only an eight-bar fugato on an invented tune.

How it was written and revised

  • The three tunes, the flute descant, the horn’s question, the woodwind answers and every decision about which tune sounds in which key and when were written by hand. A program realises only the plain string harmony under the hymn and the march’s off-beat accompaniment from a chord plan, and makes the octave doublings.
  • Four playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Checks during writing found bell, horn, oboe and flute notes outside the instruments’ ranges; all were moved.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the second movement jumped straight from one extra key to the full collision, so eight bars were added in which three keys sound at once (fiddle tune in D, bass in C, trio strain in F) with bells.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges and notes longer than the samples can sustain, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play, triplets included. The final scores report no errors. Checks for half-step clashes and parallel motion were switched off: tunes in different keys at once are the point of the piece.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished piece. With the harmonic checks off, nothing here measures whether the collisions sound like Ives or merely like mistakes.
  • The largest loss is the one described at the top: Ives’s layers are made of tunes the audience knows. Invented tunes cannot carry that memory.
  • Ives’s bands also play at different speeds. Here every layer shares one tempo and one bar line; the only displacement is a tune entering a bar late.
  • The plain harmony under the tunes is written by a program and is regular. The piece is short.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples, so the layers may not be equally audible in playback; parts can be muted in the player to hear one tune at a time. Playback uses General MIDI samples, with a tubular-bell sample for the bells. The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 2/4, 4/4 and 4/4 here).
