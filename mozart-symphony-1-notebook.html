The composition notebook

How Mozart’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a rediscovered work by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. Composed on November 16, 2026, for a request in the Missing Works voting queue.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the recorded messages, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony in a key Mozart left empty

Mozart wrote two symphonies in minor keys, both in G minor. He never wrote one in C minor, the key of his Piano Concerto No. 24, the wind serenade K. 388 and the great Mass. This is a compact symphony in that key, in four movements, lasting about ten minutes (10:03 in the site’s playback); his own late symphonies are two to three times as long.

The orchestra is that of the later version of the G minor Symphony K. 550: flute, oboes, clarinets, bassoons, horns and strings, with no trumpets or drums. The score has ten staves and is written at sounding pitch.

I. Allegro

C minor · 4/4 · 144 bars with the exposition repeated · about 4:22

The first theme is quiet: the violins rise a fifth from C to G, then turn chromatically around G (A flat, G, F sharp, G) over pulsing violas. The full orchestra answers with arpeggios that lead to the dominant of E flat. The second theme, in E flat major, falls gracefully through the triad; the violins have it first, then clarinet and flute. Scales and a full cadence close the exposition, which is repeated.

The development gives the first theme in F minor and B flat minor, returns through loud arpeggios, and waits on the dominant with the theme’s chromatic turn alone. In the recapitulation the flute has a new line above the first theme, and the second theme comes back in C minor, not C major, so the movement stays dark to its close.

II. Andante

A flat major · 3/4 · 40 bars · about 1:40

An eight-bar song for the violins, repeated by clarinet and flute. A middle section in F minor begins quietly, grows loud for four bars and falls back to the dominant. The song returns with passing notes added and the flute doubling the violins an octave higher, and four bars close the movement.

III. Menuetto: Allegretto

C minor, trio in C major · 3/4 · 72 bars · about 1:43

A sturdy minuet for the full orchestra. Its second strain turns quietly to E flat for four bars before four loud bars return to C minor; both strains are repeated. The trio, in C major, belongs to clarinet and flute over the strings, with a short second strain for the violins. The minuet is then played again without repeats.

IV. Finale: Allegro assai

C minor · 4/4 · 92 bars · about 2:18

A scurrying theme that circles C before running down the scale, first quiet in the violins, then loud in the full orchestra. The second theme, in E flat, descends by step; it is given to the violins and then to clarinet and flute. The development takes the main theme through F minor and B flat minor and stands for eight bars on the dominant. In the recapitulation the second theme returns in C minor, and the coda ends the symphony in the minor.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Mozart or anyone else.
  • The G minor Symphony K. 550 is the main model: a quiet opening over pulsing violas, no trumpets or drums, and second themes that return in the tonic minor in the first movement and finale. Its themes were avoided on purpose: this first theme does not use its repeated falling half-step, and this finale does not open with a rising arpeggio.
  • The chromatic turn around the fifth degree and the flat sixth falling to the fifth are common to Mozart’s C minor works. The eight-bar phrases, the wind repetition of each string theme and the minuet with a major-key wind trio are general Classical practice.

How it was written and revised

  • The melodies, the chord of every bar and the plan of each movement were written by hand. A program then writes the accompaniment from the chord plan in three standard textures: quiet (theme in the violins over pulsing violas, held second violins and bass strokes), woodwind (theme in clarinet and flute over the same strings) and full orchestra (repeated-note strings, sustained winds and horns, flute and oboe doubling the theme). Every note in the source carries its sounding pitch.
  • Two playable draft revisions were published. The first contained all four movements, because the program produces whole movements at once.
  • Revision after the first draft: the flute’s new line above the first theme in the first movement’s recapitulation, and the passing notes in the return of the Andante’s song.
  • Before the first draft the accompaniment’s registers were changed: the cellos had been placed too low, doubling the basses at the unison, and the second violins sat on or above the tune.
  • Balance: the printed dynamics of wind, horn and double-bass parts are two or three levels below the strings, using the adjustment measured for an earlier orchestral work on this site, which uses the same samples. These marks are a playback compromise, not dynamics for live players. The parts of this symphony were not measured one by one.

What was tested

  • A checker reads the built score and reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and minor seconds or ninths held for a quarter note or more. It also compares its reading, note by note, with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors and no warnings.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted both published revisions.
  • All four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser with all ten parts. Each engraves, plays with no notation warnings or console errors, and has no silent second.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony.
  • The accompaniment is formulaic because a program wrote it from a chord plan. The inner parts never answer the tune, the oboe only doubles in the loud passages, and every quiet passage uses the same viola figure. Mozart’s part-writing is far more alive than this.
  • There are no slurs, staccato marks or ornaments beyond a few passing notes, and dynamics are terraced.
  • The orchestration has not been tried by players; ranges were checked against simple limits only.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples, with one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 3/4, 3/4 and 4/4 here).
