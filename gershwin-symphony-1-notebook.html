The composition notebook

How Gershwin’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by George Gershwin. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony from the composer of rhapsodies

George Gershwin wrote no symphony. His concert works are two rhapsodies, a piano concerto, a tone poem, an overture and a set of variations, and most of them put a solo piano in front of the orchestra. This piece is an honest counterfactual: a short “Symphony in B flat” in three movements, fast, slow, fast, like the Concerto in F, with the piano moved back into the orchestra as one colour among others. It lasts about eight minutes (8:03 in the site’s playback). No theme is taken from Gershwin.

The orchestra: pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, alto saxophone, four horns, three trumpets, three trombones and tuba, timpani, piano and strings, on fifteen staves at sounding pitch. There is no drum kit, banjo or other percussion, because the site’s player has no such sounds.

The language, and what is lost

Melodies flatten the third and seventh of the scale against major harmony (the “blue notes”); chords carry added sixths, ninths and thirteenths; the accompaniment stabs the Charleston rhythm, one chord on the beat and one just before the third beat; basses walk in quarter notes; the slow movement is a twelve-bar blues. Because blue notes clash with their chords by design, the half-step and parallel-motion checks used for the earlier works were switched off here.

What cannot be carried over is swing. The player performs rhythms exactly as written, so the eighth notes are even, and nothing bends, slides or lays back. Much of what makes this idiom itself lives in exactly those things.

I. Allegro

B flat · 4/4 · 80 bars, exposition repeated · about 3:41

Four bars of stabbed chords set the Charleston rhythm. A clarinet has the first theme, syncopated, with flattened sevenths and a third that slides from minor to major; the full orchestra repeats it with the saxophone. A chromatic climb leads to the second theme, a broad tune in E flat for the violins over held horns and piano arpeggios, repeated an octave higher with flute and saxophone. The development gives the first theme’s opening in D flat and E flat, hands it to the piano alone over a walking bass, and lets the saxophone remember the broad tune quietly in G flat. In the reprise the broad tune returns in B flat for everyone, and the coda ends on a stab.

II. Blues: Andante con moto

E flat · 4/4 · 48 bars · about 2:40

Three choruses of a twelve-bar blues. A solo trumpet plays the melody over pulsing strings, plucked double basses walking in quarter notes, and piano. The alto saxophone takes the second chorus, and a flute answers in the gaps between its phrases. A livelier middle in A flat for clarinet and flute over Charleston stabs separates this from the third chorus, for the whole orchestra. The trumpet closes alone, with one last darkened chord.

III. Allegro agitato

B flat · 2/4 · 112 bars · about 1:42

A rondo on a theme of hammered repeated notes. Between its statements the earlier music returns at the finale’s speed: the first movement’s syncopated theme on the clarinet; the whole blues on the alto saxophone; and finally the broad tune of the first movement, grandly, in B flat for the full orchestra, as the big tune returns at the end of the Concerto in F. The repeated notes and three chords end the symphony.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Gershwin or anyone else.
  • The plan follows the Concerto in F: a first movement on a Charleston rhythm with a broad second tune, a blues slow movement that opens with a solo trumpet, and a finale of repeated notes that recalls the earlier themes and ends with the big tune. Those resemblances of layout are intended; the themes, keys and details are different, and there is no solo piano.

  • The orchestra with saxophone follows “An American in Paris”.

How it was written and revised

  • The tunes, the blues, the flute’s answers and the chord of every bar were written by hand. A program writes the rhythm-section stabs, walking basses, piano figuration and full-orchestra textures from the chord plan. The finale’s returns reuse the earlier melodies note for note, two of its bars to each of theirs.
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the double basses under the Blues were changed from bowed to plucked for their walking line, and the alto saxophone, which had very little to do in the first movement, was added to both full statements of its main theme.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges and notes longer than the samples can sustain, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors. Half-step clashes and parallel motion were not checked, for the reason given above.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all three movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser, including the saxophone and piano samples: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished piece. With the harmonic checks off there is less evidence than usual that the blue notes sound blue and not wrong.
  • No swing, no slides, no drum kit: see above. The sampled trumpet is open, not muted, and the saxophone and plucked bass are generic samples.
  • It is short, and its harmony is simpler than his: the chords have the right added notes but few of his chromatic side-slips.
  • The accompaniment is written by a program from the chord plan and repeats its patterns. Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 4/4 and 2/4 here).
