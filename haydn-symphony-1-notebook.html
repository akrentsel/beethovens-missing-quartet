The composition notebook

How Haydn’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Joseph Haydn. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

One more for London

Joseph Haydn wrote more than a hundred symphonies, so nothing is missing from his list in the way a symphony is missing from Bach’s. This is simply a new one in his late manner: four movements in B flat major, with a slow introduction, a first movement that makes almost everything out of a single theme, and the jokes he liked to play with silence and with false returns. It lasts about twelve minutes (12:18 in the site’s playback); his London symphonies are about twice as long.

The orchestra is his London band without clarinets: flute, two oboes, two bassoons, two horns and two trumpets in B flat, timpani and strings. The score has eleven staves at sounding pitch. Horns and trumpets are written as natural instruments, with only the notes of their harmonic series, and the timpani play only B flat and F. The trumpets and drums are silent in the slow movement, where the horns change to F.

F′ · E♭′ D′ — B♭ | C′ — E♭′ — A

The theme of the Allegro: a dotted fall from the fifth to the keynote, answered a step higher.

I. Adagio – Allegro

B flat major · 3/4, then 4/4 · 120 bars, exposition repeated · about 5:03

Two loud bars for the full orchestra, then ten quiet ones in which the strings sink into B flat minor and come to rest on the dominant seventh. The Allegro’s theme is given quietly by the first violins over a murmuring figure; the full orchestra answers with running scales that drive to F major. There, as often in Haydn, the “second subject” is the first theme again, now in oboe, flute and bassoon; a gentler continuation with a shadow of F minor and a loud closing group end the exposition.

The development begins by dropping the theme without warning into A flat, then gives it in C minor. The scales return in C minor, G minor and D minor, violins and basses answering each other bar by bar. Then the oboe plays the theme in E flat, as if the reprise had arrived in the wrong key; the orchestra corrects it and waits on the dominant. In the real reprise the flute adds a new line above the theme, the scales are turned to stay in B flat, and after the closing group the theme is heard once more, very quietly, before the last chords.

II. Adagio cantabile

F major · 3/4 · 42 bars · about 2:25

An eight-bar song for the first violins over a rocking accompaniment, repeated by the flute with horns holding the harmony. The middle section takes the song’s rhythm into F minor: loud for the whole band, a quiet answer in A flat, loud again, and a pause on the dominant. The song returns in violins and flute over running sixteenth notes, and a short coda says goodbye twice.

III. Menuet: Allegretto – Trio

B flat major, Trio in E flat · 3/4 · 68 bars · about 2:33

A sturdy menuet for the full orchestra. Its second part wanders quietly through C minor and stops short on an unfinished chord; after a silence the opening returns loud. The Trio is a country dance in E flat for a solo oboe, built on broken chords over plucked-style strings; the flute joins an octave higher in its second part. The menuet is then played again without repeats.

IV. Finale: Presto

B flat major · 2/4 · 136 bars · about 2:17

A rondo. The tune is a contredanse whose second strain trails off, note by note, into a bar of silence before the tune bursts back in. The first episode, in F, sets running sixteenth notes for the full orchestra against a reply from the two oboes. The second is a storm in G minor with a calm answer in B flat, and finds its way home through another silence. After the last return the coda whispers the tune’s first bar twice before the final chords.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Haydn or anyone else.
  • The plan and the habits are those of the London symphonies in general: the slow introduction that turns to the minor, the monothematic exposition, the false reprise, the general pauses, the oboe trio in the manner of a ländler, the contredanse rondo.
  • B flat is the key of his symphonies No. 98 and No. 102. This symphony follows neither in its themes; the key was chosen for the natural horns and trumpets in B flat.

How it was written and revised

  • Every tune, the chord of every bar and the form of each movement were written by hand, with the flute’s extra line in the reprise and the bass answers in the development. A program then scores each passage in one of three textures: a tune over quiet strings, a woodwind band, or the full orchestra. It writes the accompaniment figures, the sustained wind chords and the natural brass and drum parts from the chord plan.
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Problems found while writing: the site’s notation library mis-timed whole-bar rests after the change from 3/4 to 4/4 (they are now written as two half rests); doubled lines were being folded into range note by note, which broke the flute part into leaps (lines are now moved by whole octaves); held accompaniment chords clashed with the tune where the harmony changed in mid-bar (they are now re-struck at the change); and the first builds had about twenty-five parallel octaves or fifths between tune and bass, removed by changing inversions and a dozen melody notes.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: in the development the basses and bassoons, which had only repeated one note, now answer the violins’ scale figure in alternate bars, and a bassoon was added under the second subject.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes lasting a quarter note or more, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. A second check counts parallel fifths and octaves between first violins and bass. The final scores report none of either.
  • Horn, trumpet and timpani parts were checked against the notes the natural instruments have.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony. Whether the jokes are funny is exactly what the checks cannot tell.
  • The accompaniment comes from three textures written by a program, so the inner parts are regular: repeated eighth notes in every tutti, the same rocking figure under every quiet tune. Haydn’s inner parts take part in the argument far more.
  • The movements are short, and only the first has a real development. There are no slurs or staccato marks, and dynamics are terraced.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples. Playback uses General MIDI samples: one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos, single timpani strokes, modern horn and trumpet sounds.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (3/4, 3/4, 3/4 and 2/4 here).
