The composition notebook

How Bernstein’s Missing Quartet was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for two violins, viola and cello, not a work by Leonard Bernstein and not endorsed by anyone connected with him. Composed on November 15–16, 2026, for the top-voted request in the Missing Works queue.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the recorded messages, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished quartet by ear when this page was written.

One motto, four movements

The string quartet plays almost no part in Bernstein’s output. This piece asks what his manner might sound like with four string players: additive metres, stacked fourths and fifths, a big tune, a jazz movement, and themes that come back transformed in later movements.

E   A   D   B

Two rising fourths and a falling third.

Everything grows from this figure. In the first movement it is a 7/8 fanfare and, slowed down, the start of the lyrical second theme. In the scherzo it is the call of a blues riff. In the slow movement its two fourths open the song, and its mirror image (two falling fourths, a rising third) drives the middle section. In the finale it becomes a dance, and the song returns over it.

The quartet lasts about twelve minutes (11:56 in the site’s playback).

I. Allegro con brio

A major · 7/8 (2+2+3) with 5/8 and 3/4 · 106 bars · about 2:49

A two-bar curtain-raiser gives the motto to the violins in octaves and its inversion to viola and cello, then plants two stacks of open fifths a step apart (G, then A). The first theme rides a plucked cello figure: first violin, then viola with a descant above. Played in even eighth notes the motto makes a 5/8 bar, and the transition passes that bar up through all four instruments before slipping a half step off course (A flat, B flat) and landing on C.

The second theme (C major, 3/4, slower) is the motto’s two fourths sung slowly. The viola has it first, over a swaying pizzicato; the first violin repeats it with the second violin in thirds and sixths.

The development is a fugato on the first theme, with entries rising by fifths. A quiet interlude then tries the two themes against each other, because both begin with the same two fourths, and the climax plays them together at full volume in F. The recapitulation brings the second theme back in A; a short coda ends on an A major chord with an added sixth.

II. Scherzo: Riffs

D minor · 12/8 swing · 70 bars · about 2:20

A twelve-bar minor blues over a plucked walking bass. The call is the motto in swing rhythm; second violin and viola answer in parallel fourths and thirds, with blue notes (A flat, and F sharp against F). The form is laid out like a small-band chart: a head, a written-out solo chorus for the first violin, a chorus in which second violin and viola trade two-bar solos, then a hushed fugato whose subject is the motto with a blues tail, answered a fifth higher against a counter-line that walks down by step. A stretto over the dominant brings the blues back with the call an octave higher, and the movement ends with a plucked chord of D minor with added sixth and ninth.

III. Andante con tenerezza

F major · 5/4 (3+2), middle section in 3/4 · 49 bars · about 4:07

The song opens with the motto’s two rising fourths (C, F, B flat) and falls a third. Its eight bars include a Lydian B natural near the end and close through the minor subdominant. The second violin sings it first over a rocking viola; the first violin repeats it with a viola counter-melody.

The middle section turns the motto upside down and passes it upward from viola to second violin to first violin to cello while the harmony falls by fifths (D minor, G minor, C minor, F minor, B flat minor). At the climax the song’s opening returns in the minor over a dominant pedal and turns to the major within two bars. The song then continues quietly, and the cello sings all of it once more with the viola plucking the bass.

IV. Finale: Allegro giocoso

A major · 6/8 against 3/4, with two bars of 7/8 · 132 bars · about 2:39

A rondo. The refrain is the motto as a dance in which 6/8 bars alternate with bars of three quarter notes; while the tune is in one grouping the accompaniment claps the other. The first episode (C major) sets the slow movement’s song in long notes over plucked strings, first in the first violin, then in the viola with a descant. The second episode is a fugato in F sharp minor on the refrain, each entry against a counter-line that crosses its rhythm; it climbs back through two bars of the first movement’s 7/8 theme. In the coda the violins play the song broadly over the dance, the first movement’s curtain-raiser returns, and the last cadence steps F, G, A.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Bernstein or anyone else. The only quotations are of this quartet’s own themes in later movements.
  • The motto was chosen to stay clear of his best-known melodic signatures: it does not use the rising tritone that resolves up a half step (“Maria”, “Cool”), and the slow movement’s song does not open with the rising seventh of “Somewhere”.
  • Additive and changing metres (7/8, 5/8, 5/4) follow the general manner of the Candide overture, the Chichester Psalms and the Serenade. Stacks of fourths and fifths, a flat seventh degree moving to the tonic, and added sixths and ninths are common to Bernstein and to Copland.
  • The scherzo’s plan of riffs plus a quiet jazz fugue is modelled on the idea of Prelude, Fugue and Riffs and of the “Cool” fugue in West Side Story. The subject, counter-line and blues choruses are new.
  • The finale’s alternation of 6/8 and 3/4 is the huapango rhythm Bernstein used in “America”. It is a traditional rhythm; the tune here is new.
  • Bringing a slow movement’s tune back at the end of a finale over faster music is a device he used on the stage and in concert works; the same is true of many other composers.

How it was written and revised

  • Each movement was written in a private source format in which every note carries its sounding pitch; a compiler writes the ABC notation with the accidentals a reader expects. Recapitulations and rondo returns are generated from the same blocks, transposed by interval where needed.
  • Seven playable draft revisions were published during the work, starting with the first fourteen bars. They remain available from the draft page.
  • First drafts of the four movements came to just under ten minutes. The revision pass added new music rather than repeats: an interlude in the first movement’s development and the viola’s statement of the second theme in its recapitulation; a chorus of traded solos in the scherzo; a last strophe for the cello in the slow movement; a longer coda in the finale. Tempos of the scherzo and slow movement were eased slightly.
  • Things the checks caught and that were fixed before publication: the very first sketch had the three upper parts an octave too high; a metre change written at the start of a line was mis-timed by the notation library; a five-eighth rest could not be notated as one symbol; in the finale sharps were missing from two viola bars; and about twenty-five places where two parts sat a minor second or minor ninth apart for an eighth note or longer were re-voiced. A cello chord in the recapitulation fell below the instrument’s range after transposition and was rewritten.

What was tested

  • A checker written for this quartet reads the source and reports bar lengths under every metre change, instrument ranges, the reach of double stops, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and minor seconds and ninths between parts. It also compares its own reading, note by note, with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors and no warnings.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every published revision (one tune per movement, full bars, aligned parts).
  • All four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser. Each engraves, plays with no notation warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in the full mix. Rendered lengths: 169 s, 140 s, 247 s and 159 s.
  • Each part was also rendered alone on the first drafts. The four instruments came out at similar levels (in movement I, raw RMS 0.044, 0.043, 0.032 and 0.039 from first violin to cello), and the plucked passages produce sound.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished quartet. Buffer measurements show that sound is produced and roughly how loud it is; they say nothing about whether the swing swings or the tune sings.
  • Jazz-inflected chords and parallel motion are deliberate here, so the classical voice-leading rules used for the earlier works were not applied. The dissonance check is a filter for likely accidents, not a judgment of the harmony.
  • Playback uses General MIDI string samples. The scherzo’s swing is written as 12/8 and has no jazz articulation; pizzicato is one generic plucked-string sample for all four instruments; accents and hairpins have little or no effect; fast notes can sound soft.
  • Double stops were checked with a rule of thumb for hand positions, and no string player has tried the parts.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (this affects every work on the site). The first movement begins in 7/8, the scherzo in 12/8, the slow movement in 5/4 and the finale in 6/8; later metre changes are printed.
  • The blues choruses share their lower parts and the finale’s refrain returns unchanged; some listeners may want more variation there.
