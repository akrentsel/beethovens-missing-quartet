The composition notebook

How Vivaldi’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Antonio Vivaldi. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

One more sinfonia, with a soloist

Antonio Vivaldi is the one Baroque composer in this library who did write pieces called sinfonia: dozens of short works for strings and continuo in three movements, fast, slow, fast, close cousins of the Italian opera overture and of his own concertos for strings. They are not symphonies in the later sense, but they are among the ancestors of the form. This piece is a new one of that kind, in G minor. It takes one liberty: a solo violin, as in his concertos, leads the first violins in the tuttis and has episodes of its own. It lasts about seven minutes (6:55 in the site’s playback), the scale of his own sinfonias.

The score has seven staves: solo violin, first and second violins, violas, cellos, double basses and harpsichord. In the slow movement the orchestral strings play pizzicato.

G G G · G — D′ | E♭′ E♭′ E♭′ · D′ — C′

The opening of the first movement, played by all the strings in octaves.

I. Allegro

G minor · 4/4 · 73 bars · about 2:31

The ritornello has three parts: four bars of a hammering theme for every string instrument in octaves; six bars in which both violin parts run in sixteenth notes down a chain of falling fifths over a marching bass; and a two-bar cadence with a trill. The unison theme returns in B flat major and in D minor, the sequence and cadence in G minor. Between these returns the solo violin has four episodes: broken chords that move to B flat; a singing sequence that turns into running notes; arpeggios round the circle of fifths; and a last flourish over a held D in the basses while the other strings hold quiet chords. The whole ritornello closes the movement, its cadence echoed softly and then repeated loud.

II. Largo

E flat major · 3/4 · 40 bars · about 2:24

The solo violin sings a slow melody while the orchestral strings pluck chords after each downbeat and the harpsichord breaks the harmony into single notes. The first twelve bars, from E flat to B flat, are heard twice: plainly, and then with the solo line decorated in the way a player of the time would have improvised. The second part passes through C minor and A flat and closes in E flat.

III. Presto

G minor · 3/8 · 80 bars, both halves repeated · about 2:00

A driving dance in two repeated halves, built from one eight-bar theme and sixteenth-note sequences. The first half moves to B flat, where the theme is played quietly in the major; the second climbs to D minor, falls back through the circle of fifths, waits over a held D, and returns to the theme and a hammered close in G minor.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Vivaldi or anyone else.
  • The models are his string sinfonias and concertos without soloist, and for the solo writing his violin concertos: ritornello form with a unison opening, sequences on the circle of fifths, broken-chord solo figuration, a slow movement for a soloist over a reduced accompaniment, and a short triple-time finale in two halves.
  • Plucked accompaniment under a singing solo violin is a texture he used in several slow movements. The melody here is new.

How it was written and revised

  • The themes, the Largo’s melody and its decorated repeat, the first four bars of the second solo and the chord of every beat were written by hand. A program writes the rest from the chord plan: the broken-chord figuration of the solo episodes and sequences, the inner parts, the basses and the harpsichord. For each passage of figuration it tries several arpeggio patterns and keeps the first that makes no parallel fifths or octaves with the bass.
  • Two playable drafts were published.
  • Checks during writing found parallel octaves and fifths at the joins of the Presto theme (two bars of the theme were changed).
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the Largo’s first strain was a plain repeat and is now written out a second time with decoration; the first movement’s final cadence gained a quiet echo; and two statements of the Presto theme were made piano so that the movement is not loud throughout.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes lasting a quarter note or more, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. A second check counts parallel fifths and octaves between the top line and the bass outside the deliberate unison passages. The final scores report none of either.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted both revisions, and all three movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished piece.
  • Much of the surface is figuration written by a program from a chord plan. That is closer to Vivaldi’s own practice than it would be for most composers, but his figuration is shaped for the violin’s strings and bow, and this is not; there are no slurs, bowings or double stops.
  • The harmony keeps to the commonest sequences. The surprises of his best movements are missing.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples. Playback uses General MIDI samples: a solo-violin sample, one string-ensemble sample for the orchestral strings, and a generic plucked-string sample in the Largo. The player’s trills are the only ornaments beyond the written decoration.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 3/4 and 3/8 here).
