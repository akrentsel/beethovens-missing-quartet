The composition notebook

How Stravinsky’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Igor Stravinsky. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony made of blocks

Igor Stravinsky used the word “symphony” for several quite different works, from the early Symphony in E flat to the Symphony in C and the Symphony in Three Movements. This is a new, short “Symphony in G” in the manner of his neoclassical years. It lasts about eight minutes (8:04 in the site’s playback); his own symphonies of that period run twenty to thirty. No theme is taken from him.

The orchestra: pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, four horns, two trumpets, three trombones, timpani, piano and strings, on fourteen staves at sounding pitch. The piano is a percussive member of the orchestra, as in the Symphony in Three Movements.

The method

The music is not developed in the nineteenth-century way. It is built from short blocks, each in one metre and one texture, placed side by side without transitions and brought back in a different order. The pulse stays constant while the metre changes. The harmony is mostly white-note, with added seconds, sixths and sevenths; the strings are dry and detached, and the winds carry much of the tune. Half-step clashes inside chords and parallel motion are part of this style, so those two checks were switched off for this work.

I. Allegro

G · 4/4, 3/4 and 2/4 in alternation · 94 bars · about 2:13

Four kinds of block: a motto of three repeated notes and a leap for the full orchestra with piano; a rocking figure for oboes and flutes over horns, in three-four; the motto again, dry, for violins over detached chords; and a trumpet fanfare in two-four over hammering strings. A clarinet song in D over held strings and a block of syncopated chords close the first part. The middle throws the fanfare a third higher into B flat and the rocking figure into E flat, then makes a short fugato of the motto (violins, violas, cellos and basses), and hammers four bars of dominant chords with holes in them. The blocks return in order, the song now in G for flute, and the movement ends with the motto, a bar of silence and one chord.

II. Andante

E minor · 2/4 and 3/4 · 48 bars · about 2:00

Brass, timpani and piano are silent except for the horns. The strings are plucked. A flute traces an arabesque of long notes and quick turns; a clarinet answers a fourth lower. The strings take their bows for a plain eight-bar chorale in three-four with horns. Plucked again: flute and clarinet in sixths, then the arabesque in G for oboe with a bassoon two octaves below. Half of the chorale returns, and the movement ends plucked, with the flute.

III. Allegretto

C · 2/4 and 3/8 in alternation, 3/4 in the waltzes · 92 bars · about 1:31

One six-bar cycle is played thirteen times: two bars of two-four, two of three-eight, one of two-four, one of three-eight, with the eighth note constant, so that the beat keeps tripping. It is heard from oboes and flutes, from the full orchestra, dry in the strings, from clarinets, and twice from the piano alone in octaves over plucked basses; sometimes a third higher or a fourth lower. Twice it stops for a mock-waltz, first for bassoon, then for clarinet. Three chords with a bar of silence between them end it.

IV. Largo – Allegro – Largo

G · 4/4, then 2/4 and 3/4 · 76 bars · about 2:20

A slow chorale for oboes, trumpets, flutes, clarinets, horns and trombones, its chords coloured by added seconds and sixths. The Allegro sets a tune over a dry ostinato, interrupts it with four bars of spaced chords in three-four, and repeats both for the full orchestra and, in D, for clarinets. The first movement’s motto of three repeated notes returns and climbs. After a last statement of tune and chords the tempo drops, and the chorale returns quietly, now with the strings, to end the symphony.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Stravinsky or anyone else.
  • The models are the Symphony in C and the Symphony in Three Movements: Classical proportions heard through displaced accents and changing metres, the orchestral piano, and construction by juxtaposed blocks.
  • Ending a symphony with a slow, quiet chorale for winds is the gesture of the Symphony in C and, earlier, of the Symphonies of Wind Instruments. The chorale here is new.

How it was written and revised

  • The tunes, the fugato, the chorale, the order of the blocks and the chord of every bar were written by hand. A program writes the ostinato accompaniments, held chords, piano chords and full-orchestra textures from the chord plan, one block at a time.
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Two errors of metre in the source (melodies read in the metre of the neighbouring block) were caught by the bar-length check before anything was published.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the third movement was the shortest and had only three colours, so two cycles for the piano alone over plucked basses were added.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths under every change of metre, instrument ranges and notes longer than the samples can sustain, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors. Half-step clashes and parallel motion were not checked, for the reason given above.
  • The changes between plucked and bowed strings in the second movement use the method verified for the Ravel symphony in this batch (the instruction is placed where the site’s notation library honours it).
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony.
  • Stravinsky’s rhythm lives on accents, and the site’s sampled playback barely renders accents, so the displaced stresses that should make the blocks bite will sound flatter than written.
  • It is short and its blocks are simple. The harmony is milder than his: there are added notes but few of the sharp bitonal collisions of his own scores.
  • The accompaniment inside each block is written by a program from the chord plan and is regular.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples. Playback uses General MIDI samples. The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 2/4, 2/4 and 4/4 here), though it prints the later changes.
