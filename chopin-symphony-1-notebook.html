The composition notebook

How Chopin’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Frédéric Chopin. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony from a composer who never left the piano

Frédéric Chopin wrote no symphony. Every piece he published includes a piano, and the handful with orchestra (two concertos and four shorter works) treat the orchestra as a frame for the soloist. So this piece cannot pretend to continue a tradition of his. It is an honest counterfactual: what might four of his genres become if an orchestra had to play them with no pianist? It is new music. It is not an orchestration of any piano piece, and no theme is his. It lasts about thirteen minutes (13:04 in the site’s playback).

How the piano is translated

  • The singing right hand goes to a clarinet, a flute or the violins.
  • Its decoration, which Chopin wrote as small notes and which grows at each return of a melody, is written out here in triplets and sixteenth notes.
  • The left hand’s wide broken chords go to a harp, over held strings. The harp is my addition to the orchestra of his concertos (pairs of woodwind, four horns, two trumpets, timpani, strings); he never wrote for it.
  • The drone fifths of his mazurkas go to cellos, basses and horns; the off-beat chords of dance accompaniments to second violins and violas.

The score has thirteen staves at sounding pitch.

I. Allegro maestoso

B minor, ending in B major · 4/4 · 108 bars, exposition repeated · about 5:47

A grave theme in dotted rhythm for the full orchestra, with a turn to the flattened second degree before its cadence, is repeated quietly by the clarinet with a first ornament. Rising broken chords lead to D major and the second theme, a nocturne: the clarinet sings over harp and held strings, and violins and flute repeat the melody with the decoration a pianist’s right hand would add. A closing theme ends the exposition.

The development takes the main theme through D minor and G minor and lets the transition storm; a B flat in the bass is re-read as A sharp and opens the way home. As in Chopin’s second and third piano sonatas, the reprise leaves out the main theme and begins with the transition, turned towards B major, where the nocturne theme and the closing theme return. Only in the coda does the main theme come back, transformed into the major, before a quiet close with harp.

II. Nocturne: Larghetto

G major · 12/8 · 36 bars · about 2:53

Trumpets and timpani are silent. The first violins play a long melody over harp arpeggios and held strings; the flute repeats it with added turns. In the middle the violins have an agitated recitative in G minor over trembling strings, as in the slow movement of his F minor Concerto. The melody returns in violins and flute with still more decoration, and four bars of farewell glance once more at the minor.

III. Tempo di mazurka

B minor, Trio in B major · 3/4 · 72 bars · about 2:20

A mazurka in place of a scherzo: a dotted first beat and the weight on the later beats of the bar, with the accompaniment’s chords placed there. A brighter strain in D for oboe and flute separates two statements. The Trio, slightly slower, sits on a drone of B in the bass with horns and harp; the clarinet’s tune raises the fourth of the scale to E sharp, as Polish folk music and his own mazurkas do. The mazurka returns without repeats.

IV. Rondo à la krakowiak: Vivace

B major · 2/4 · 120 bars · about 2:04

The krakowiak is a Polish dance in duple time with a short–long–short syncopation; Chopin used it for the finale of his E minor Concerto and for a concert rondo. After a horn-call introduction in B minor for clarinet, horns and harp, the dance tune is played by the violins and repeated by the full orchestra. One episode is in F sharp for oboe and clarinet, followed by running sixteenth notes; the other is a storm in B minor with a gentle answer in D. The coda drives the rhythm to the end.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Chopin or anyone else, and no passage is transcribed from a piano score.
  • The forms are his: a first movement whose reprise omits the first theme (Sonatas Nos. 2 and 3); a nocturne whose melody is more decorated at each return and has an agitated middle; a mazurka with a drone trio; a krakowiak rondo. B minor to B major is the plan of the Third Sonata’s first movement; the themes here are different.
  • The harmony uses his favourite colours: the flattened second degree before a cadence, the minor subdominant in a major key, and one enharmonic change (B flat becoming A sharp).

How it was written and revised

  • The melodies, their ornamented versions and the chord of every bar were written by hand. A program writes the harp figuration, the held chords, the dance accompaniments, the full-orchestra textures and the brass from the chord plan.
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Checks during writing found 24 parallel octaves or fifths between first violins and bass, removed by changing chord inversions and reordering a few melody notes, and a sustained E sharp against F sharp in the Trio, shortened to a passing note.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the Nocturne’s last return was given a new, more elaborate ornamented line, so that the decoration grows from statement to statement; and the harp, which had fallen silent after the second movement, was added to the Mazurka’s Trio and to two passages of the finale.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes lasting a quarter note or more, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play, triplets included. A second check counts parallel fifths and octaves between first violins and bass. The final scores report none of either.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished piece.
  • The largest loss in translation is rubato. Chopin’s melodies depend on a flexible beat that a sampled player in strict time cannot give, and the ornaments here are in exact rhythm.
  • The decoration is modest: turns and short runs, not the long free cascades of small notes in his nocturnes. The harmony is simpler and less chromatic than his.
  • The harp and inner parts are written by a program from the chord plan, so the accompaniment is regular. Whether a harp is a convincing stand-in for his left hand is a matter of taste; it is my choice, not his.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples. Playback uses General MIDI samples (one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos). The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 12/8, 3/4 and 2/4 here).
