The composition notebook

How Dvořák’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a rediscovered work by Antonín Dvořák. Composed on November 16, 2026, for the top-voted request in the Missing Works queue.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the recorded messages, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished symphony by ear when this page was written.

A compact symphony

This is a symphony in B minor in the four-movement plan Dvořák used: a sonata-form Allegro, a slow movement, a furiant for scherzo, and a finale that gathers up earlier themes and ends in the major. It is much shorter than his own symphonies, about eleven and a half minutes (11:26 in the site’s playback) against thirty-five to forty-five.

The score has twelve staves: flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon, horn, trumpet, timpani, first and second violins, violas, cellos and double basses. Pairs of wind players share a staff where two notes are written. There are no trombones. The score is written at sounding pitch throughout, including clarinets, horns, trumpets and double basses.

B   F♯   E D C♯   D   B   A

The main theme: a rising fifth, a stepwise fall, and a flat seventh.

I. Allegro maestoso

B minor · 4/4 · 118 bars · about 3:56

Cellos and bassoon give the theme quietly over a held horn fifth and plucked basses; the full orchestra repeats it. A transition passes the theme’s first bar between strings and woodwind down to the dominant of D. The second theme, in D major, uses only five notes (D, E, F sharp, A, B) and a short-long rhythm; flute and clarinet play it in thirds, then the violins. A striding closing theme ends the exposition.

The development takes the theme’s opening through D minor (clarinet, bassoon) and G minor (oboe, flute), climbs from C minor to E minor, and settles on a long dominant pedal where the second theme appears in the minor. The recapitulation brings the second theme and closing theme back in B major, and the coda returns to B minor with the theme in horns, bassoon and cellos.

II. Adagio

G major · 4/4 · 45 bars · about 3:02

A four-bar chorale for clarinets, horns and bassoon frames the movement. The song is for solo oboe and is pentatonic; the violins repeat it with the clarinet a third below. The middle section, a little faster and in G minor, follows the pattern of a dumka: a clarinet lament over plucked strings, a lighter dance in B flat for flute and oboe, then the lament in the full strings. The cellos bring the song back under a flute descant, and the chorale closes the movement.

III. Scherzo: Furiant

D major · 3/4 · 120 bars · about 2:08

A furiant groups pairs of 3/4 bars into three broad beats before the metre straightens out. The tune does this twice in each eight-bar phrase. It is played by the full orchestra, then quietly by flute and clarinet over plucked strings, then in B minor and E minor, and returns after a dominant pedal. The trio, slower and in G major, is a lilting tune for clarinets in thirds over a cello drone, with a second strain for the strings. The scherzo is then played again in full.

IV. Finale: Allegro con fuoco

B minor, ending in B major · 2/4 · 143 bars · about 2:20

After four bars of drum and low strings the violins play a stamping dance tune, which the full orchestra repeats. The second theme, in D major, is a broad melody for cellos and clarinet under pulsing violins, then for violins and woodwind. In the development the dance tune’s first two bars enter one above another; horns and then trumpets recall the first movement’s theme at half speed; the oboe recalls the Adagio’s song. The dance returns, the second theme follows in B major, and in the coda trumpets, horns and the bass instruments play the first movement’s theme at half speed in B major, keeping its flat seventh, while the strings hold the dance rhythm. A faster major-key version of the dance ends the symphony.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Dvořák or anyone else. Themes recur only within this symphony.
  • Pentatonic tunes with a short-long snap follow the manner of his American-period works (the Ninth Symphony, the “American” Quartet). The Adagio’s plan (a wind chorale as frame, a solo reed song, a faster minor-key middle) is modelled on the Largo of the Ninth. The chords, the tune and the solo instrument (oboe, not English horn) are different, and the tune was written to stay clear of the Largo’s melody.
  • The furiant follows the type he used for the scherzo of the Sixth Symphony and in the Slavonic Dances. The dumka pattern of lament and dance comes from the “Dumky” Trio and other chamber works.
  • Recalling earlier movements in the finale follows the Ninth. The flat seventh degree in the main theme, and a major chord on it in the last coda, are modal habits of his.
  • The main theme’s rising fifth and dotted rhythm belong to a common type of Romantic horn-call theme; it is not derived from a particular Dvořák theme.

How it was written and revised

  • Each movement was written in a compact private source in which every note carries its sounding pitch. A build step writes the twelve-staff ABC score, fills silent parts with rests, creates doublings from one written line (for example double basses an octave below the cellos), and reuses blocks for recapitulations and the scherzo’s return, transposed where needed.
  • Nine playable draft revisions were published during the work, starting with the first sixteen bars; the last matches the published score. They remain available from the draft page.
  • Balance: rendering each part alone showed that the site’s wind, brass and double-bass samples are louder than its string-ensemble sample at the same marking. The printed dynamics of those parts are therefore one to three levels below the strings (flute and double bass three; oboe, clarinet, bassoon and horn two; trumpet one), and solo passages are marked up to compensate. These marks are a playback compromise, not dynamics for live players.
  • Other revisions after the first complete draft: moving violas in place of held notes at the start of the first movement’s development, and a flute descant over the cellos’ return of the song in the Adagio.
  • Things the checks caught and that were fixed before publication: a second-violin doubling and several double-bass doublings that fell below the instruments’ ranges, flute doublings above its range, a handful of sustained minor seconds and ninths between parts, and a tempo mark that was being printed on every staff.

What was tested

  • A checker reads the built score and reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and minor seconds or ninths held for a quarter note or more. It also compares its reading, note by note, with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors and no warnings.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every published revision.
  • All four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser with all twelve parts. Each engraves, plays with no notation warnings or console errors, and has no silent second.
  • Each of the twelve parts was rendered alone to measure its level; see the balance note above.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony. Buffer measurements show that sound is produced and roughly how loud each part is; they do not show whether the orchestration sounds like an orchestra.
  • The orchestration has not been tried by players. Ranges were checked against simple limits; breathing, bowing, brass stamina and balance in a hall were not. The timpani part uses four pitches in the outer movements (B, F sharp, A, D), which needs four drums or quick retuning.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples: one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos, a generic plucked-string sample for pizzicato, and single-note timpani strokes with no rolls. Long notes can run out after about six seconds; none here is longer than five.
  • The harmony is deliberately plain and diatonic, with few inner counter-melodies; much of the tutti writing doubles the tune in octaves over repeated-note harmony. A real Dvořák score is busier inside.
  • The movements are short and the developments brief. This is a symphony in outline more than in scale.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 4/4, 3/4 and 2/4 here).
