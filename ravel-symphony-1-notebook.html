The composition notebook

How Ravel’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Maurice Ravel. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony from a composer who avoided the word

Maurice Ravel wrote no symphony. His orchestral music is ballet, dance and orchestration; his abstract multi-movement works are chamber music, the Sonatine and two piano concertos. This piece is therefore an honest counterfactual: a compact “Symphonie en ré” that takes the four-movement plan of his String Quartet — a moderate first movement, a quick scherzo with plucked strings and a slow middle, a very slow movement, and a fast finale in an uneven metre — and writes new music on that plan for his kind of orchestra. It lasts about nine and a half minutes (9:32 in the site’s playback). The brevity is deliberate. No theme is taken from Ravel.

The orchestra: two flutes, two oboes, cor anglais, two clarinets, two bassoons, four horns, two trumpets, harp, celesta and strings, on fourteen staves at sounding pitch. There are no timpani or percussion.

The language

Modes replace major and minor (the Dorian mode on D, with its raised sixth, in the first movement; the Aeolian on A in the second). Chords of the seventh and ninth, and chords with an added sixth, are treated as points of rest. Melodies are doubled at the octave by unlike instruments. Because of this, two of the checks used for the earlier works in this batch were switched off: half-step clashes inside chords and parallel fifths and octaves are part of the style.

I. Modéré

D Dorian, ending in D major · 3/4 · 100 bars · about 3:08

A calm theme for clarinet with the flute an octave above, over quiet seventh chords and harp; the violins take it up. Four rising bars lead to F and the second theme, for cor anglais over harp and held strings, repeated by violins and flute with celesta. The short development gives the first theme to a solo flute a third higher, lets its first two bars climb by minor thirds until the full orchestra reaches a ninth chord, and subsides. In the reprise the second theme returns in D major, and the coda dissolves the first theme into a chord with added sixth and ninth.

II. Assez vif, très rythmé

A minor · 6/8 · 96 bars · about 1:52

The strings are plucked throughout the fast music. The first violins and harp have a theme of rising broken chords, repeated by flute and clarinet; it climbs twice by a third, and the whole orchestra answers with three beats against the bar’s two. In the slow middle section the strings take their bows: the cor anglais sings over seventh chords, and violins and flute answer with celesta. The plucked scherzo returns, with the cross-rhythm once more, and ends with a plucked close.

III. Très lent

B flat major · 3/4 · 50 bars · about 2:47

Trumpets are silent. After two bars of accompaniment alone (a bass note, then two soft chords, like a waltz slowed almost to stillness), a solo flute plays a sixteen-bar melody. A warmer middle section for violins, oboe and cor anglais rises to the movement’s one climax. The melody returns in the violins with the flute an octave above and celesta, and four bars close on a chord with added sixth and ninth.

IV. Vif

D major · 5/8 (three plus two) · 88 bars · about 1:45

A darting theme for violins and harp, repeated by the full orchestra, and a second idea in longer notes for cor anglais, then violins, flute and celesta. The theme’s first four bars are heard in F, A flat and B flat; four bars on the dominant bring it back. The second idea returns on the clarinet in D Dorian, the mode of the first movement, and the coda repeats the opening bar to the last chord.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Ravel or anyone else.
  • The plan follows the String Quartet in F: its second movement is a scherzo with plucked strings and a slow middle, and its finale is in five-eight time. Those two features are borrowed openly; the keys, modes and themes are different.
  • A long melody over a slow waltz-like accompaniment is the idea of the Adagio of the Piano Concerto in G. Here it is in B flat for flute, and the melody is new.
  • The scoring habits (cor anglais solos, harp and celesta figuration, octave doublings) are his in general.

How it was written and revised

  • The melodies and the chord of every bar were written by hand. A program writes the harp and celesta figuration, the held chords and the fuller textures from the chord plan.
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: in the slow movement the cor anglais, clarinets and bassoons had nothing to play and were given the tune’s lower octave and held harmony; the celesta was added to the finale; one passage of the finale ran above the violins’ range and was lowered an octave.
  • A playback fault was found and fixed after the revision: the instruction that changes the strings from plucked to bowed for the scherzo’s slow middle section was being ignored by the site’s notation library, because it stood at the start of a line. It is now written at the end of the preceding line, and the library’s audio sequence was checked: the first violins’ notes carry the plucked sound, then the bowed sound, then the plucked sound again.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths (including five-eight), instrument ranges and notes longer than the samples can sustain, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors. Half-step clashes and parallel motion were not checked, for the reason given above.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished piece. With the usual harmonic checks switched off, there is less evidence than for the earlier works that the chords sound as intended.
  • Ravel’s orchestration is among the most detailed ever written; this has none of his special effects (harmonics, divided strings, mutes, glissandi), and the sampled celesta, harp and plucked strings are generic.
  • The harmony is modal and uses his chord types, but it moves in plain four- and eight-bar phrases; his sliding chromatic inner parts are missing.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples. The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (3/4, 6/8, 3/4 and 5/8 here).
