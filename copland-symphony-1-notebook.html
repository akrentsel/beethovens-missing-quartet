The composition notebook

How Copland’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Aaron Copland. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

One more symphony in the plain style

Aaron Copland wrote three numbered symphonies and a Dance Symphony. This is a new “Symphony in D” in the open, plain-spoken manner of his Third Symphony and of the ballets he wrote in the 1940s. It lasts about nine and a half minutes (9:28 in the site’s playback); his Third lasts over forty. No theme is taken from Copland, and no folk tune, hymn or cowboy song is quoted. In particular, the brass fanfare that opens the finale is new: it is not the “Fanfare for the Common Man”, which he built into the finale of his own Third Symphony.

The orchestra: pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, four horns, three trumpets, three trombones and tuba, timpani, piano and strings, on fourteen staves at sounding pitch.

The language

Melodies are built from fourths and fifths. A line is often stated first with nothing under it, in bare octaves. Chords are plain major triads with an added second or fourth, spaced widely; one instrumental colour is heard at a time. Because added-note chords and parallel fifths are the vocabulary, the half-step and parallel-motion checks used for the tonal works in this batch were switched off here.

A — D′ | E′ — — D′ | B — E′ | A

The first four bars of the main theme: a rising fourth, a step, a rising fourth again.

I. Molto moderato

D · 4/4 · 62 bars · about 3:46

Flute, clarinet and strings play the theme alone, in octaves. The violins repeat it over plain chords with added seconds while the horns hold. Oboe and violas bring a second, more flowing idea. The theme is heard darker, in F, for clarinets and bassoons, then a step higher in G for violins and flute, and reaches a climax in D for trumpets and trombones over trembling strings, timpani and piano. The flowing idea returns quietly, and the theme’s first rise is heard twice more before an open fifth.

II. Allegro molto

D · 2/4 with bars of 3/4 · 92 bars · about 1:43

Four stamped bars on an open fifth, then a fiddle-style dance on the five-note scale, for violins and then the whole orchestra with piano. It is interrupted by four bars of spaced chords in three-four, continues in A for clarinet and flute, and returns in D. The trio is slower: a quiet tune in G for the clarinet over held strings, repeated by violins and flute. The dance comes back, is heard once more a fourth higher, is interrupted again, and ends with its first two bars.

III. Andantino

A · 4/4 · 36 bars · about 1:54

Trumpets, trombones and timpani are silent. A solo flute plays an eight-bar melody over held strings. It plays it again, and the oboe follows a bar later and a fifth lower, in canon. A small dance for clarinet, with the bassoon an octave below, makes the middle. The melody returns in the violins with the flute above and the oboe’s canon beneath, the horns holding and the piano adding bell-like fifths, and four bars close on an open fifth.

IV. Molto deliberato – Allegro risoluto – Maestoso

D · 4/4, 2/4, 4/4 · 68 bars · about 2:05

A slow fanfare for trumpets over horns and trombones, the timpani answering. The quick movement that follows makes its theme from the first movement’s rising fourth and fifth; it is played by the violins and the full orchestra, makes room for the dance of the second movement on clarinet and flute, and is heard in G and again in D. Then the tempo broadens, and the first movement’s theme returns for the whole orchestra with trumpets and trombones on the tune. The second half of the fanfare ends the symphony.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Copland or anyone else, and no folk material is used. Copland often borrowed real tunes; this piece does not.
  • The plan follows his Third Symphony: a broad first movement that opens with a theme in octaves, a quick second movement, a quiet third that begins with a high solo line, and a finale that starts from a brass fanfare and ends by bringing back the opening theme of the symphony. Those resemblances are intended; the themes are new.
  • The fiddle-style dance follows the manner of his ballet scores; a flute melody answered in canon is in the manner of his quiet pastoral music.
  • A fanfare for brass with timpani answers, built on fourths and fifths, will remind any listener of the “Fanfare for the Common Man”. This one begins with a falling fifth, not a rising fourth and fifth, and shares none of its phrases; but the family resemblance is unavoidable and is acknowledged here.

How it was written and revised

  • The themes, the canon, the fanfare, the timpani answers and the chord of every bar were written by hand. A program writes the held chords, dance accompaniments, piano and full-orchestra textures from the chord plan. The finale’s closing section reuses the first movement’s theme and chords exactly.
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the oboe’s canon had been dropped when the flute melody returned in the third movement and was restored there; the second movement, the shortest, was given one more statement of the dance, a fourth higher, before its last interruption.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths under each change of metre, instrument ranges and notes longer than the samples can sustain, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors. Half-step clashes and parallel motion were not checked, for the reason given above.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony.
  • Copland’s plainness is exact: the spacing of every chord and the choice of every doubling are what make it sound like him. Here the chords are spaced by a program, and the result may sound merely plain.
  • His quick music depends on irregular accents and shifting metres far more than this does; only the second movement changes metre, and the sampled playback hardly renders accents.
  • It is short, and there is no percussion beyond timpani, because the site’s player has none.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples. Playback uses General MIDI samples. The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 2/4, 4/4 and 4/4 here).
