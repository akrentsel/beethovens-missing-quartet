The composition notebook

How Glass’s Missing Quartet was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for two violins, viola and cello, not a work by Philip Glass and not endorsed by him. Composed on November 16, 2026, for a request in the Missing Works voting queue.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the recorded messages, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

Cycles and processes

This quartet has four short movements and lasts about nine minutes (9:02 in the site’s playback), close to the scale of Glass’s String Quartet No. 2, “Company”. It is made the way much of his music is described: a small number of chord cycles, repeated figuration, and audible processes in which a pattern gains or loses a note at each return.

The movements were not written note by note. A short program holds the chord cycles, the figuration patterns and the plan of repetitions, and writes out the four parts. The musical decisions are in that plan: which chords, how the arpeggios mirror each other, where a part enters or leaves, how the bars grow.

Movement I

G minor · 4/4 · 72 bars · about 2:34

A cycle of eight chords, two bars each: G minor, E flat and C minor over a held G, D over F sharp, G minor, B flat, E flat, and D with a suspended fourth that resolves. The viola plays each chord as a rising and falling arpeggio; the second violin joins with the same arpeggio upside down. On the second pass the first violin adds one long note to a bar. A middle section in B flat sets three notes in the second violin against four in the viola. On the third pass the first violin moves in half notes and the cello walks in quarter notes; then the texture thins to the viola and stops on G minor.

Movement II

E flat major · bars of three to six beats · 39 bars · about 2:48

Over a rocking viola and held notes in second violin and cello, the first violin plays a rising scale across four chords (E flat, C minor, A flat, B flat). Each time the four chords come round the scale has one more note, so the bars lengthen from three beats to four, five and six. In the middle the scale turns downward in C minor and the bars shorten again from six beats to three. The opening returns briefly and rests on E flat.

Movement III

C minor · 6/8, 7/8, 8/8 · 76 bars · about 1:24

The violins play interlocking three-note arpeggios while the viola plays three even strokes to the bar and the cello holds the bass of a four-chord cycle (C minor, A flat, F minor, G). The cycle is played in bars of six eighths, then seven, then eight, each time louder. A middle section in E flat gives the first violin long notes. The cycle returns at its longest and shrinks back through seven eighths to six, ending quietly.

Movement IV

G minor · 4/4 · 59 bars · about 2:16

The viola begins alone on two notes, G and B flat, then takes up the first movement’s arpeggio and its eight-chord cycle. The first violin climbs a scale to the top note of each chord, two notes the first time, then three, and one more for each chord until the scale fills the bar; the next pass is loud, with the scale in every bar. The last pass is quiet. The first violin holds its top notes and stops, the second violin stops, and the viola ends alone on G and B flat before a final chord of G minor.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No music is quoted from Glass or anyone else.
  • The models are his string quartets, especially No. 2 (“Company”), No. 3 (“Mishima”) and No. 5: rocking arpeggios in the inner parts, slow lines above, cycles of a few chords, and three against two. The key of “Company” and its chord cycles were avoided on purpose.
  • Lengthening and shortening a pattern by one unit at a time is the additive process of his early works, applied here to a scale in the second movement and to the bar length in the third.
  • Chord cycles of this kind (for example a minor chord, the major chords a third and a sixth above its root, and the dominant) are common property of minimalist and film music. They were chosen as a style, not taken from a particular piece.

How it was written and revised

  • A generator program writes each movement’s private source from the chord cycles, the figuration patterns and the repetition plan; a build step turns that source into the ABC score. Every note in the source carries its sounding pitch.
  • Two playable draft revisions were published. The first contained all four movements, because the generator produces a whole movement at once.
  • Revision: in the first draft the third movement returned to its opening and repeated the same stretching from six to seven to eight eighths, ending loudly. It now returns at eight and shrinks back to six, ending quietly, so the movement mirrors itself. Rests in the last movement were also tidied.

What was tested

  • A checker reads the built score and reports bar lengths under every metre change, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and minor seconds or ninths held for a quarter note or more. It also compares its reading, note by note, with what the site’s notation library will play, including the triplets. The final scores report no errors and no warnings.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted both published revisions.
  • All four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser. Each engraves, plays with no notation warnings or console errors, and has no silent second.
  • On the first draft each part was also rendered alone. The four instruments came out at similar levels (in Movement I, raw RMS 0.034, 0.042, 0.035 and 0.047 from first violin to cello).

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished quartet. In music this repetitive, small things decide whether it holds attention: bow changes, slight accents, the blend of the arpeggios. Sampled playback gives none of that, and the checks cannot judge it.
  • The repetition is literal. A player would shape each return; the samples play every bar the same way.
  • No slurs or bowings are marked, and dynamics are terraced, one level per section.
  • The fast arpeggios may sound soft or blurred with the site’s bowed-string samples.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 3/4, 6/8 and 4/4 here); later metre changes are printed.
