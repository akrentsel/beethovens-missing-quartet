The composition notebook

How C. P. E. Bach’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony of interruptions

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach, the second son of Johann Sebastian, wrote about nineteen symphonies. The best known are from his Hamburg years: six for strings and four “for twelve obbligato parts”. They are short, in three movements played without a break, and they behave like no one else’s: themes for the whole orchestra in octaves that stop dead, hushed answers, keys that change without preparation, silences in the wrong places. This piece is a new symphony in that manner, in A minor. It lasts a little over six minutes (6:16 in the site’s playback); his own run ten to twelve.

The orchestra is close to that of the Hamburg orchestral symphonies: two flutes, two oboes, bassoon, two natural horns in C, strings and harpsichord, ten staves at sounding pitch.

A — E′ · C′ | G♯ · B · E — | (silence)

The opening, played by the whole orchestra in octaves, which breaks off after four bars.

I. Allegro di molto

A minor · 4/4 · 76 bars · about 2:25

The whole orchestra states a jagged theme in octaves and stops. The strings answer in a hush, with sighing figures. An outburst of tremolo arpeggios over a striding bass leads to C major and a tender second idea of wide leaps for violins and flute. Then, without warning, the unison theme returns in D minor. Its answer follows, and a tremolo sequence climbs through B flat, C and D minor to a halt on the dominant. There the music is interrupted by four bars of strange calm in F major for two flutes and soft strings, as if from another piece. In the reprise the second idea returns in the minor and is itself broken into by two bars of fortissimo diminished-seventh tremolo, after which it goes on as if nothing had happened. The unison theme is heard once more and dissolves quietly towards F major.

II. Larghetto

F major · 3/4 · 32 bars · about 1:55

Oboes, bassoon and horns are silent. Two flutes sing in thirds over hushed strings and harpsichord. In the middle the strings are alone, and the harmony slips through C minor, A flat and D flat before it finds F major again. The flutes’ song returns with the first violins joining it, and four bars turn aside to the dominant of A minor so that the finale can begin at once.

III. Presto

A minor · 6/8 · 64 bars, both halves repeated · about 1:56

A headlong finale in two repeated halves. Racing broken chords for the full orchestra move from A minor to C major, and a closing phrase begins quietly and ends loud. The second half opens with the theme in E minor in octaves, falls through a sequence of fifths, and is suddenly quiet for eight bars in which the two flutes move in thirds and sixths above the violins. The theme returns in A minor, and scales for everyone in octaves end the symphony.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from C. P. E. Bach or anyone else.
  • The models are the Hamburg symphonies in general: three linked movements, unison openings, sudden pauses and changes of dynamics, unprepared changes of key, a short slow movement that ends on a dominant, a quick triple-time finale. The outbursts that interrupt quiet music, and the quiet music that interrupts outbursts, are the habit of his “sensitive style”.
  • The flutes in thirds over hushed strings follow the slow movements of the orchestral symphonies.

How it was written and revised

  • The themes, unisons, the flute duets, the interruptions and the chord of every beat were written by hand. A program scores the quiet and loud passages from the chord plan: accompaniment figures, sixteenth-note tremolo, wind chords, horn notes and harpsichord.
  • Two playable drafts were published.
  • Checks during writing found thirteen parallel octaves or fifths between tune and bass, most of them where a broken chord in the Presto ended on the bass note of the next bar, and a missing natural sign in the last bars of the Larghetto.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the two-bar fortissimo interruption in the first movement’s reprise was added, and in the Presto’s quiet episode the flutes, which had doubled the violins, were given their own lines in thirds and sixths.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes lasting a quarter note or more, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. A second check counts parallel fifths and octaves between first violins and bass outside the unison passages. The final scores report none of either. The horn parts were checked against the notes of the natural horn in C.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted both revisions, and all three movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony. Its effect depends on surprise, which is exactly what checks cannot measure.
  • It is short, and the surprises are placed where his usually are; his own are stranger.
  • The accompaniment textures are written by a program from the chord plan and are regular. The written-out silences are whole rests in tempo; there are no pauses of free length.
  • The movements are separate files in the player, so the links between them are heard only if the next movement is started by hand.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples. Playback uses General MIDI samples, with one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos. The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 3/4 and 6/8 here).
