The composition notebook

How Debussy’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a work by Claude Debussy and not a rediscovered score. Composed on November 17, 2026, for a top-voted request in the Missing Works queue.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the recorded messages, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony Debussy did not write

Debussy left no orchestral symphony; the nearest things are an early symphony movement for piano four hands and the three “symphonic sketches” of La Mer. This homage is a short symphony in D flat in four movements, about nine minutes long (9:10 in the site’s playback). It borrows his habits more than his forms: melodies on five-note and whole-tone scales, chords that slide in parallel, shimmering accompaniments, solo woodwind, harp, and themes that return in later movements.

The score has fifteen staves at sounding pitch: flute, oboe, cor anglais, clarinet, bassoon, horns, trumpets, timpani, harp (two staves) and strings.

F · A♭ B♭ · D♭ E♭ F

The call: six rising notes of the five-note scale, which then fall back.

I. Très modéré

D flat major · 6/8 · 64 bars · about 2:42

Violas murmur on two notes over a held bass while the harp rises through the chord. The cor anglais plays the call, and the horn repeats it a fifth lower. The flute has the first theme, a floating tune with one raised note (the Lydian fourth); the first violins and oboe repeat it with the flute above. The tempo quickens a little as the call bends into the whole-tone scale over a low pedal; whole-tone waves rise through the strings with a muted trumpet, and the climax is four major chords descending by whole steps. After two held-back bars the theme returns in the violins with the cor anglais playing the call beneath it, and the horn’s call closes the movement.

II. Assez vif et léger

E flat Dorian · 9/8 · 64 bars · about 2:05

A light dance for the oboe, then for flute and clarinet, over harp triads and plucked basses. A second idea has the strings moving in parallel fourths over a stepwise bass; the full orchestra takes it up with a muted trumpet tapping the rhythm. In the middle the cor anglais has a long melody over harp and held strings, repeated by flute and first violins. The dance returns in plucked violins, then oboe and clarinet, the second idea comes once more, and the tune breaks into fragments before a last plucked chord.

III. Lent et expressif

B flat minor · 4/4 · 26 bars · about 2:05

The strings move in parallel seventh chords, down the scale and back up. The flute plays a long arabesque over harp and held strings. The chords return with the cor anglais singing the arabesque’s first phrases over them, the flute floats above the second half, and the movement ends on a soft minor chord with an added ninth.

IV. Animé

D flat major · 6/8 · 84 bars · about 2:18

Over a quick shimmer the clarinet recalls the call. The main theme is a broad five-note tune, first in horns and cor anglais, then in trumpets, flute and oboe. The first movement’s theme returns in the violins and then in the woodwind with the call in the horns beneath it. The whole-tone music of the first movement comes back, rises twice, and reaches the same four descending chords. The main theme then fills the full orchestra, twice, the second time a step higher, and in a faster coda trumpets and horns broaden the call over the full shimmer before a five-note rush upward and the last chord.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Debussy or anyone else.
  • La Mer is the main model: themes shared between the first and last movements, a broad brass tune in the finale, and an opening call given to the cor anglais. In La Mer a call for cor anglais and muted trumpet plays the same role; the notes here are different, but the kinship is deliberate.
  • The cor anglais solos and the parallel chords of the slow movement follow “Nuages”; the muted trumpet tapping a rhythm in the second movement follows the distant procession in “Fêtes”; a long flute solo over harp recalls the Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune, though this arabesque is modal and not chromatic.
  • Whole-tone passages and chords moving in parallel are found throughout his piano Préludes and orchestral music. Five-note melodies reflect his interest in the gamelan he heard in Paris in 1889.
  • The four-movement plan with a separate slow movement is not Debussy’s; his orchestral works favour three panels. It was chosen because the request was for a symphony.

How it was written and revised

  • The melodies, the chord of every bar, the scoring of each section and the plan of each movement were chosen by hand. Small programs then spell out the repeated accompaniment figures (the shimmer between two notes, the harp arpeggios, the held and plucked basses) from the chord plan. The music was written in easier keys a half step lower and transposed by the build, so every note in the published score is at sounding pitch in D flat.
  • Six playable draft revisions were published, starting with the first twenty bars; the last matches the published score.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the tempo now moves in the first movement (a little faster for the whole-tone music, held back before the theme returns) and the finale’s coda is faster; the cor anglais plays the call under the returning theme in the first movement; “con sord.” and “senza sord.” were added to the trumpet part.
  • Balance: each of the fifteen parts was rendered alone, first for the opening and then for all four movements. The harp and the solo winds were lowered after the first measurement, and the flute and harp were lowered again in the finale’s loud passages after the second. The final version was not measured part by part again. As in the other orchestral works on this site, wind, brass and double-bass dynamics are printed one to three levels below the strings; these marks are a playback compromise, not dynamics for live players.
  • Things the checks caught and that were fixed before publication: the horn’s first call lay too high and was moved a fifth lower; several flute, oboe and bassoon doublings went out of range when sections were transposed; and the checker itself was erasing the harp’s brace from the score layout before comparing parts, which made it compare the wrong staves.

What was tested

  • A checker reads the built score and reports bar lengths in 6/8, 9/8 and 4/4, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and minor seconds or ninths held for a dotted quarter note or more. It also compares its reading, note by note, with what the site’s notation library will play. The final scores report no errors and no warnings.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every published revision.
  • All four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser with all fifteen parts, including the harp and cor anglais samples, which no earlier work on the site uses. Each engraves, plays with no notation warnings or console errors, and has no silent second.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony. Debussy’s orchestral writing lives on colour and balance, which is exactly what sampled playback and buffer measurements cannot judge.
  • The accompaniment is uniform. One two-note shimmer figure serves most of three movements; a real score would vary it constantly, divide the strings, and use tremolo, harmonics and harp glissandi. None of those is here.
  • The trumpet is marked muted, but the playback sample is an open trumpet. Pizzicato is one generic sample, and the timpani have single strokes only.
  • Dynamics are terraced with no hairpins, and there are no slurs or bowings.
  • The orchestration has not been tried by players, and the harp part was not checked for pedalling.
  • The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (6/8, 9/8, 4/4 and 6/8 here).
