The composition notebook

How Handel’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by George Frideric Handel. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A symphony from a composer of concertos and suites

George Frideric Handel wrote no symphony. His orchestral music is the concerti grossi, the organ concertos, the overtures to his operas and oratorios, and the suites for outdoor occasions. This piece is an honest counterfactual: a four-movement Sinfonia in F major put together from those manners. It lasts about nine minutes (9:19 in the site’s playback).

The band is that of the F major music for the river: two natural horns, two oboes, bassoon, strings and harpsichord, eleven staves written at sounding pitch. The horn parts use only notes of the harmonic series of F. To keep it distinct from the Bach symphony written in the same batch, there is no fugue here: the four movements are built on ritornello, song, dance and variation.

F — E — D — C — B♭ — B — C

The ground bass of the finale.

I. Largo – Allegro

F major · 4/4 · 74 bars · about 2:48

Eight stately bars for the whole band close on the dominant. The Allegro is an Italian ritornello movement. Its eight-bar ritornello climbs the F major triad, answers itself a step higher, and ends with running turns and a trilled cadence. It returns shortened in C, in D minor and in B flat, and complete in F at the end. Between the returns: two oboes in thirds over the bassoon; the first violins in running arpeggios over the other strings; a short link for strings and held oboes; and a passage over a dominant pedal in which horn calls are answered by the oboes.

II. Larghetto

D minor · 3/4 · 24 bars, both halves repeated · about 2:29

Horns, second oboe and bassoon are silent. The first violins play an air in sarabande rhythm, with its weight on the second beat, over a slow bass. In the second half a solo oboe takes the tune through F major and G minor; when the opening returns, violins and oboe play it together.

III. Alla Hornpipe

F major · 3/2 · 44 bars · about 1:57

A hornpipe with the syncopation of the dance: a short note, then a long one across the beat. Both strains are repeated. A quiet middle section in D minor is for strings alone. The hornpipe returns without repeats, its first four bars given to oboes, bassoon and horns before the strings rejoin.

IV. Chaconne

F major · 3/4 · 72 bars · about 2:05

Seventeen variations on a four-bar bass that steps down from F to C and climbs back through B flat and B natural. The theme is heard from the strings and then from the full band. The variations pass it through flowing eighth notes, oboes in thirds, sixteenth notes, horn calls, a running bass and a dotted tune. Four variations are in F minor, with a lament for the solo oboe. The major returns with the theme decorated, horns and oboes in dialogue, and the full band over the running bass.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Handel or anyone else.
  • The ritornello Allegro follows the concerti grossi; the scoring with horns in F and the hornpipe in 3/2 follow the Water Music. That suite’s famous hornpipe is in D major with trumpets; this one is a different tune in F, and only the metre and the syncopated rhythm of the dance are shared.
  • The sarabande-like air and the major–minor–major chaconne follow his keyboard suites and organ concertos.

How it was written and revised

  • Themes, episodes, horn parts, the slow movement’s bass, the ground and the chord of every beat were written by hand. A program writes the score from that source: doublings, the filling inner parts, repeated-note basses in the fast movements, the harpsichord, and the arpeggio figuration of four passages (the violin episode of the first movement and three chaconne variations).
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Checks during writing found the ritornello’s tune moving in octaves with the bass (one bar changed), parallel fifths at the start of the Hornpipe (re-harmonised), and three cadences in the Larghetto where the filling parts held a chord against the tune (re-timed).
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the first movement was extended with a string link and a fourth ritornello in B flat, and the Hornpipe’s return was re-scored so that it is not a literal repeat.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes between parts lasting a quarter note or more (in the Hornpipe, longer than a quarter note, because its tune uses quarter-note passing notes). It compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. A second check counts parallel fifths and octaves between tune and bass; the final scores have none, apart from one place in the Larghetto where the first violins change from an inner part to the tune.
  • The horn parts were checked against the harmonic series of the natural horn in F.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony.
  • It is short: about nine minutes, where a Handel concerto grosso runs twelve to fifteen.
  • Inner parts, repeated-note basses, the harpsichord and four passages of arpeggio figuration were written by a program from the chord plan, so they are regular where Handel’s would be inventive. The chaconne variations change texture but never the harmony of the ground.
  • Dynamics are written as intended for players and were not altered to balance the samples; the horns and harpsichord may sound stronger in playback than in a real band. Parts can be muted in the player.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples (a modern horn, one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos). The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 3/4, 3/2 and 3/4 here).
