The composition notebook

How Schumann’s Missing Symphony was written, checked and revised.

An original AI-composed homage for orchestra, not a lost, rediscovered or authentic work by Robert Schumann. Composed on November 18, 2026 as part of a catalogue-building batch for the Missing Works library.

This is an edited, high-level account based on the composition log, score revisions and test results. It is not a verbatim transcript or a disclosure of private internal reasoning. The musical judgments are analytical. The composing agent inspected notation, note data and synthesised audio buffers; it did not listen to the music, and no person had reviewed the finished work by ear when this page was written.

A fifth symphony, held together by four notes

Robert Schumann finished four symphonies, in B flat, C, E flat and D minor. This is a new one in F minor that ends in F major. Like his D minor Symphony it is cyclic: a short motto heard at the start returns in every movement, and themes from the first movement come back transformed in the last. It lasts about sixteen minutes (15:41 in the site’s playback). No theme is taken from Schumann.

The orchestra is that of his later symphonies: pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, four horns, two trumpets, three trombones, timpani and strings. The score has thirteen staves at sounding pitch; the four horns share one staff and the three trombones another.

F — A♭ — G — C′

The motto: up a third, down a step, up a fourth.

I. Ziemlich langsam – Lebhaft

F minor · 4/4 · 132 bars, exposition repeated · about 5:39

Horns state the motto quietly over trombone chords; the strings extend it and a crescendo leads to the fast movement. Its main theme is the motto in dotted rhythm. The full orchestra drives it to A flat major, where the clarinet has a long-breathed second theme, taken over by violins and flute, and the exposition closes with the motto triumphant in the major.

The development gives the main theme in B flat minor and C minor, recalls the second theme far away in D flat, and then makes a fugato of the motto: first violins, seconds, violas and basses enter a bar apart. After a wait on the dominant the reprise brings the main theme for the full orchestra and the second theme in F major. The major does not hold: the coda falls back to F minor and ends with the motto in octaves.

II. Romanze: Andante

D flat major · 3/4 · 54 bars · about 2:34

Trumpets and timpani are silent. A clarinet sings a sixteen-bar song with a bassoon an octave below, over quiet string chords placed after the beat. In the middle, horns over three trombones play the motto as a chorale in B flat minor; the strings answer; the chorale turns to D flat. The song returns in violins, flute and oboe, and a short coda touches the minor once before it closes.

III. Scherzo: Lebhaft – Trio

F minor, Trio in D flat · 3/4 · 120 bars · about 3:02

A scherzo of rising and falling broken chords, quiet in the strings and then loud for everyone. Its second part gives the figure to the clarinet in D flat and B flat minor, then hammers it for the full orchestra over a held C and breaks off into silence. The Trio is a calmer song in D flat for clarinet and flute, taken by the strings into B flat minor and brought home by the winds. The scherzo returns without repeats, beginning this time in clarinet and flute, and a short coda ends it.

IV. Langsam – Lebhaft

F major · 4/4 · 144 bars · about 4:26

Eight slow bars: the motto very quietly in the strings, still in the minor, then with the brass, growing. The finale’s main theme is the first movement’s theme, and so the motto, turned note for note into F major, as a march for the full orchestra. After a transition to C the second theme of the first movement returns on the oboe, and the motto closes the exposition as a fanfare. The development lets the theme fall back into the minor (A minor, D minor) and answers with the motto in long notes as a chorale for the whole orchestra. The reprise stays in F, and a faster coda ends the symphony on the motto.

Influences, and what is not quoted

  • No melody is quoted from Schumann or anyone else.
  • The model is the D minor Symphony (No. 4): a slow introduction that returns as a bridge to the finale, a Romanze, themes carried from the first movement into the last, a minor-key work that ends in the major. The Romanze of that symphony is famous for a melody played by oboe and cello in octaves. This one deliberately uses a different pair, clarinet and bassoon, and a different tune, but the idea of a melody doubled at the octave by two unlike instruments is his.
  • The solemn trombone chorale follows the fourth movement of the “Rhenish” Symphony in spirit. Dotted rhythms, off-beat accompaniments and thick doubling of melodies are his habits throughout.

How it was written and revised

  • The motto and its transformations, the themes, the fugato, the chorales and the chord of every bar were written by hand. A program then scores each passage in one of three textures (a tune over quiet strings, a woodwind band, the full orchestra) and writes accompaniment figures, tremolo, wind and brass chords and timpani from the chord plan. The finale’s main theme is literally the first movement’s theme read in F major instead of F minor.
  • Three playable drafts were published, starting with the first movement.
  • Checks during writing found a misplaced trombone entry in the fugato, a G natural that should have been G flat in the Scherzo, and eleven parallel octaves or fifths between first violins and bass, three of them under the finale’s chorale, whose bass was re-harmonised.
  • Revision after the first complete draft: the oboe had nothing to play in the Romanze and now doubles the violins in its return, and the Scherzo’s reprise was re-scored for clarinet and flute so that it is not a literal repeat.

What was tested

  • A checker reports bar lengths, instrument ranges, notes longer than the samples can sustain, and half-step clashes lasting a quarter note or more, and compares its reading note by note with what the site’s notation library will play. A second check counts parallel fifths and octaves between the melody and the bass. The final scores report none of either. The timpani part was checked to use only F and C.
  • The site’s draft validator accepted every revision, and all four movements were rendered in the site’s real player in a headless browser: each engraves, plays without warnings or console errors, and has no silent second in its rendered audio. That is an inspection of audio data, not listening.

Limits and open questions

  • Nobody has listened to the finished symphony.
  • The accompaniment is written by a program in a few textures and is uniform; inner parts do not take part in the argument except in the fugato. Schumann’s characteristic syncopations across the bar line are mostly absent, because the tunes were kept to simple rhythms.
  • The cyclic returns are transpositions and changes of mode, not the freer transformations he made. The movements are separate files in the player, so the bridge from Scherzo to Finale is not heard without a break.
  • Dynamics are terraced and written as intended for players, not altered to balance the samples; the brass may sound heavier in playback than in a hall. Parts can be muted in the player.
  • Playback uses General MIDI samples, with one string-ensemble sample for violins, violas and cellos and single timpani strokes. The site’s score view does not print the opening time signature of a movement (4/4, 3/4, 3/4 and 4/4 here).
